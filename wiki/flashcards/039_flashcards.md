<details><summary>At what pubertal stage is pubertal suppression with GnRH agonists officially recommended in transgender youth?</summary>

At the confirmed onset of biological puberty, defined as Tanner stage 2 (breast budding in AFAB; testicular volume >=4 mL in AMAB); it is not initiated in prepubertal children (Tanner 1).

</details>

<details><summary>How does the reversibility of GnRH agonist pubertal suppression compare with gender-affirming sex steroid therapy?</summary>

GnRH agonist pubertal suppression is fully reversible (endogenous puberty resumes if stopped), whereas gender-affirming sex steroid therapy induces partially irreversible changes (e.g., voice deepening, breast tissue).

</details>

<details><summary>What hematologic parameter must be monitored regularly in transgender males receiving testosterone therapy to prevent polycythemia?</summary>

Hemoglobin and hematocrit; testosterone stimulates erythropoiesis, and doses should be reduced if hematocrit exceeds 50-52%.

PDF p. 1033
</details>

<details><summary>What are the therapeutic serum hormone targets for transgender females undergoing feminizing hormone therapy?</summary>

Serum 17-beta estradiol maintained between 100 and 200 pg/mL, with serum total testosterone suppressed to <50 ng/dL.

PDF p. 1031
</details>

<details><summary>What electrolyte abnormality requires routine monitoring in transgender females taking spironolactone as an antiandrogen?</summary>

Hyperkalemia (elevated serum potassium), due to spironolactone's aldosterone-antagonist effect on the distal renal tubule.

PDF p. 1034
</details>

<details><summary>When must counseling regarding fertility preservation options be conducted with transgender youth and their families?</summary>

Prior to initiating pubertal suppression (GnRHa) or gender-affirming hormone therapy (estrogen/testosterone), while gametogenesis is still intact.

</details>

<details><summary>Why is DXA bone mineral density monitoring indicated during prolonged GnRH agonist pubertal suppression?</summary>

Sex steroids are required for normal pubertal bone mineral accretion; prolonged gonadotropin suppression can delay peak bone mass acquisition, requiring calcium/vitamin D optimization.
