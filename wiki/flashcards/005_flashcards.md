<details><summary>What is the primary genetic mechanism causing Prader-Willi Syndrome in 65% to 75% of cases?</summary>

Paternal microdeletion of the 15q11.2-q13 chromosomal region; maternal uniparental disomy (UPD15) accounts for most remaining cases.

</details>

<details><summary>What pathognomonic body composition abnormality is present in infants with PWS even prior to the development of obesity?</summary>

Markedly increased percent body fat and severely reduced lean muscle mass, accompanied by decreased resting energy expenditure.

</details>

<details><summary>What diagnostic evaluation is mandatory prior to initiating somatropin in a child with PWS to prevent fatal airway complications?</summary>

Polysomnography (sleep study) and an upper airway ENT examination to evaluate for obstructive sleep apnea and adenotonsillar hypertrophy.

PDF p. 147
</details>

<details><summary>Name three absolute contraindications to starting or continuing somatropin therapy in Prader-Willi Syndrome.</summary>

Severe uncontrolled obesity, severe untreated obstructive sleep apnea, and active systemic respiratory tract infection.

</details>

<details><summary>What is the standard recommended maintenance dose of somatropin for children with Prader-Willi Syndrome?</summary>

1.0 mg/m2/day (approximately 0.035 mg/kg/day), often initiated at 0.5 mg/m2/day and titrated upward.

PDF p. 148
</details>

<details><summary>Beyond linear height, what major developmental benefits does early somatropin therapy confer on young children with PWS?</summary>

Accelerated gross motor milestone achievement, increased physical stamina, and improved expressive language and cognitive developmental quotients.

</details>

<details><summary>When should repeat polysomnography be performed after initiating somatropin in an infant or child with PWS?</summary>

Within 6 to 12 weeks after treatment initiation, or promptly if new or worsening snoring or sleep apnea occurs.
