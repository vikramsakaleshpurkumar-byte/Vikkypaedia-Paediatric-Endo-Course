## Authored MCQ

An 18-month-old toddler boy presents with marked progressive bowing of both legs (genu varum) and waddling gait since starting to walk at 14 months. Serum testing reveals: calcium 9.4 mg/dL (normal: 8.8-10.2 mg/dL), phosphate 2.1 mg/dL (normal: 4.5-6.5 mg/dL), alkaline phosphatase 950 U/L (normal: 150-400 U/L), intact PTH 55 pg/mL (normal: 15-65 pg/mL), 25-hydroxyvitamin D 38 ng/mL, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D 28 pg/mL (inappropriately normal), and TmP/GFR 1.8 mg/dL (severely reduced). His mother has short stature and bowlegs. What is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Nutritional vitamin D deficiency rickets
B. X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets (XLH)
C. Vitamin D-dependent rickets type 1A (CYP27B1 mutation)
D. Pseudohypoparathyroidism type 1A

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets (XLH)

This toddler exhibits classic X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets (XLH), an X-linked dominant disorder caused by inactivating mutations in PHEX. Hallmarks are normal serum calcium, severe hypophosphatemia due to renal phosphate wasting (reduced TmP/GFR), elevated alkaline phosphatase, normal PTH, and inappropriately normal (rather than elevated) 1,25-(OH)2D driven by elevated intact FGF23. The maternal family history of short stature and bowed legs supports X-linked dominant inheritance.

## Authored MCQ

What is the primary mechanism of action of burosumab in the treatment of pediatric X-linked hypophosphatemia?

A. Direct inhibition of intact FGF23 signaling by binding circulating FGF23, thereby restoring renal tubular NaPi-IIa/c expression and normalizing 1,25-(OH)2D synthesis
B. Allosteric activation of the calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR) in parathyroid tissue
C. Competitive antagonism of parathyroid hormone at the PTH-1 receptor in the proximal tubule
D. Stimulation of renal 24-hydroxylase (CYP24A1) to promote phosphate reabsorption

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Direct inhibition of intact FGF23 signaling by binding circulating FGF23, thereby restoring renal tubular NaPi-IIa/c expression and normalizing 1,25-(OH)2D synthesis

Burosumab is a recombinant human IgG1 monoclonal antibody that binds to and neutralizes circulating intact FGF23. By blocking excess FGF23 action at the FGFR1/Klotho complex in the renal proximal tubule, burosumab increases tubular expression of NaPi-IIa and NaPi-IIc cotransporters, enhancing renal phosphate reabsorption, and relieves suppression of CYP27B1, boosting endogenous 1,25-(OH)2D production.

## Authored MCQ

A 4-year-old girl presents with rickets, genu valgum, and severe recurrent kidney stones. Serum phosphate is low at 2.2 mg/dL with low TmP/GFR. Serum calcium is normal (9.8 mg/dL), but 24-hour urine calcium is markedly elevated at 7.5 mg/kg/day (hypercalciuria). Intact FGF23 is suppressed (<10 pg/mL) and serum 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D is markedly elevated at 145 pg/mL. What is the diagnosis and appropriate therapy?

A. X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets; treat with burosumab
B. Hereditary hypophosphatemic rickets with hypercalciuria (HHRH); treat with oral phosphate salts alone
C. Vitamin D deficiency rickets; treat with high-dose ergocalciferol
D. Primary hyperparathyroidism; perform parathyroidectomy

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Hereditary hypophosphatemic rickets with hypercalciuria (HHRH); treat with oral phosphate salts alone

Hereditary hypophosphatemic rickets with hypercalciuria (HHRH) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by biallelic mutations in SLC34A3 (encoding NaPi-IIc). Defective renal phosphate reabsorption leads to hypophosphatemia, which appropriately suppresses FGF23. Low FGF23 disinhibits 1-alpha-hydroxylase, causing massive 1,25-(OH)2D production, hyperabsorption of intestinal calcium, and severe hypercalciuria with nephrolithiasis. Treatment is oral phosphate salts alone to replenish phosphate, which suppresses 1,25-(OH)2D and cures hypercalciuria. Exogenous calcitriol is strictly contraindicated.

## Authored MCQ

A 10-year-old child with XLH has been treated for 5 years with conventional therapy consisting of high-dose oral phosphate supplements and calcitriol. Routine surveillance reveals serum calcium 9.6 mg/dL, phosphate 3.0 mg/dL, but intact PTH has risen progressively to 450 pg/mL (reference: 15-65 pg/mL). Renal ultrasound demonstrates bilateral medullary nephrocalcinosis. What complication of conventional therapy has developed?

A. Tertiary (autonomous) hyperparathyroidism secondary to chronic phosphate administration
B. Transformation of XLH into osteogenesis imperfecta
C. Development of a parathyroid carcinoma
D. Acute renal cortical necrosis

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Tertiary (autonomous) hyperparathyroidism secondary to chronic phosphate administration

Conventional therapy for XLH with oral phosphate salts causes repeated transient spikes in serum phosphate, which bind ionized calcium and stimulate parathyroid gland hyperplasia. Over years, this chronic stimulation can induce secondary and ultimately tertiary (autonomous) hyperparathyroidism with profound PTH elevation, compounded by calcitriol-induced hypercalciuria and nephrocalcinosis. This severe complication is a primary reason why burosumab has superseded conventional therapy.

## Authored MCQ

A 12-year-old boy presents with progressive bone pain, proximal muscle weakness, fatigue, and multiple metatarsal insufficiency fractures. Labs show serum phosphate 1.6 mg/dL, normal calcium, normal PTH, normal 25-OH-D, low 1,25-(OH)2D, and marked renal phosphate wasting (TmP/GFR 1.2 mg/dL). Serum intact FGF23 is markedly elevated. There is no family history of bone disease. Full-body 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT reveals an intensely avid 1.2-cm soft-tissue lesion in the distal femur. What is the diagnosis?

A. Tumor-induced osteomalacia (TIO) caused by a phosphaturic mesenchymal tumor
B. Osteosarcoma with paraneoplastic hypercalcemia
C. Fibrous dysplasia in McCune-Albright syndrome
D. Ewing sarcoma with ectopic ACTH production

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Tumor-induced osteomalacia (TIO) caused by a phosphaturic mesenchymal tumor

Tumor-induced osteomalacia (TIO) is a paraneoplastic syndrome caused by benign, slow-growing phosphaturic mesenchymal tumors (PMTs) that oversecrete FGF23. This results in severe hypophosphatemia, renal phosphate wasting, inappropriately low 1,25-(OH)2D, and severe osteomalacia/fractures. PMTs overexpress somatostatin receptors (SSTR2), making 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT the most sensitive localization modality. Complete surgical resection of the tumor cures the condition.
