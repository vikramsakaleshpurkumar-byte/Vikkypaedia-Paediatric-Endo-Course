## Authored MCQ

A 10-day-old male infant presents with generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Laboratory testing demonstrates serum total calcium 5.8 mg/dL (reference: 8.8-10.5 mg/dL), ionized calcium 0.70 mmol/L (reference: 1.15-1.32 mmol/L), serum phosphate 9.8 mg/dL (reference: 4.5-6.5 mg/dL), magnesium 1.8 mg/dL, and intact PTH 4 pg/mL (reference: 15-65 pg/mL). Cardiac evaluation reveals a conotruncal cardiac defect (truncus arteriosus). What is the underlying genetic diagnosis?

A. 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (DiGeorge syndrome)
B. Albright hereditary osteodystrophy (GNAS mutation)
C. Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (CaSR mutation)
D. X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets (PHEX mutation)

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

22q11.2 deletion syndrome (DiGeorge syndrome)

DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2 deletion syndrome) results from defective development of the third and fourth pharyngeal pouches, leading to congenital hypoparathyroidism (absent or hypoplastic parathyroid glands presenting with profound hypocalcemia, hyperphosphatemia, and inappropriately low PTH), conotruncal cardiac anomalies (truncus arteriosus, interrupted aortic arch, tetralogy of Fallot), thymic hypoplasia (T-cell immunodeficiency), and facial dysmorphisms.

## Authored MCQ

A 15-year-old asymptomatic boy is referred for incidental hypercalcemia found on a sports physical. Serum total calcium is 11.2 mg/dL, ionized calcium 1.42 mmol/L, serum phosphate 3.2 mg/dL, and intact PTH 52 pg/mL (reference: 15-65 pg/mL). A 24-hour urine collection reveals low urinary calcium with a calcium-to-creatinine clearance ratio (CCCR) of 0.005. His father and paternal grandfather also have mild asymptomatic hypercalcemia. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

A. Urgent four-gland surgical parathyroid exploration and subtotal parathyroidectomy
B. Reassurance and observation without surgical intervention
C. Intravenous zoledronic acid infusion every 6 months
D. High-dose oral furosemide and sodium chloride tablets daily

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Reassurance and observation without surgical intervention

This patient has classic familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (FHH), an autosomal dominant condition caused by a heterozygous loss-of-function mutation in the CaSR gene. Diagnostic hallmarks are lifelong mild-to-moderate hypercalcemia, inappropriately normal intact PTH, and marked hypocalciuria with a calcium-to-creatinine clearance ratio (CCCR) <0.01. FHH is entirely benign, does not cause end-organ injury (no nephrolithiasis or bone loss), and parathyroidectomy does not cure the condition. Surgical intervention is contraindicated.

## Authored MCQ

A 7-year-old girl with postsurgical hypoparathyroidism following total thyroidectomy for thyroid cancer is treated with calcitriol 0.5 mcg daily and oral calcium carbonate 500 mg elemental calcium twice daily. On follow-up, serum total calcium is 8.8 mg/dL, phosphate is 5.2 mg/dL, and a spot urine calcium-to-creatinine ratio is 0.38 mg/mg (normal <0.20). Renal ultrasound reveals bilateral medullary nephrocalcinosis. What adjustment in medical therapy is indicated?

A. Increase calcitriol to 1.0 mcg daily to bring serum calcium into the high-normal range
B. Decrease calcitriol and oral calcium to lower urine calcium excretion, targeting serum calcium of 8.0-8.5 mg/dL, and consider adding a thiazide diuretic
C. Discontinue calcitriol and start daily recombinant human parathyroid hormone injections
D. Add oral sodium phosphate supplements

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Decrease calcitriol and oral calcium to lower urine calcium excretion, targeting serum calcium of 8.0-8.5 mg/dL, and consider adding a thiazide diuretic

In hypoparathyroidism, the absence of PTH removes the primary stimulus for renal tubular calcium reabsorption. Exogenous calcium and calcitriol easily precipitate hypercalciuria (urine Ca/Cr >0.2 mg/mg) and nephrocalcinosis. Management requires reducing calcitriol/calcium to keep serum calcium in the low-normal range (8.0-8.5 mg/dL) and adding a thiazide diuretic (e.g., hydrochlorothiazide), which promotes proximal and distal tubular calcium reabsorption to reduce urinary calcium excretion.

## Authored MCQ

A newborn infant presents at 4 days of life with severe hypotonia, failure to thrive, rib fractures, and respiratory distress. Serum calcium is markedly elevated at 17.5 mg/dL, phosphate is 2.8 mg/dL, and intact PTH is 650 pg/mL. Skeletal radiographs show diffuse demineralization and subperiosteal resorption. Genetic testing confirms a homozygous inactivating mutation in the CaSR gene. What is the definitive life-saving therapy?

A. Emergent total parathyroidectomy
B. Lifelong high-dose intravenous calcium infusions
C. Oral calcitriol and dietary phosphate restriction
D. Bilateral adrenalectomy

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Emergent total parathyroidectomy

Neonatal severe primary hyperparathyroidism (NSHPT) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous loss-of-function mutations in CaSR. Without functioning calcium sensors, parathyroid cells do not recognize extreme hypercalcemia, producing massive PTH secretion and torrential skeletal resorption with fractures and respiratory failure. Emergent total parathyroidectomy (with autotransplantation of parathyroid tissue or lifelong hypoparathyroidism management) is life-saving; medical therapy alone is rarely sufficient.

## Authored MCQ

A 14-year-old boy presents to the emergency department with carpopedal spasm and facial twitching upon tapping the facial nerve anterior to the ear. What are the clinical names of these two physical signs, and what electrolyte disturbance do they indicate?

A. Trousseau sign and Chvostek sign; acute hypocalcemia
B. Kernig sign and Brudzinski sign; hypomagnesemia
C. Gowers sign and Trendelenburg sign; hypercalcemia
D. Tinels sign and Phalens sign; hyperphosphatemia

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Trousseau sign and Chvostek sign; acute hypocalcemia

Carpopedal spasm induced by inflating a blood pressure cuff above systolic pressure for 3 minutes is Trousseau sign. Twitching of facial muscles elicited by tapping the facial nerve trunk anterior to the external auditory meatus is Chvostek sign. Both are classical clinical signs of latent neuromuscular excitability (tetany) secondary to acute hypocalcemia (or profound hypomagnesemia).
