## Authored MCQ

A 10-year-old boy who completed treatment for a standard-risk medulloblastoma 3 years ago (including surgical resection and 23.4 Gy craniospinal irradiation with posterior fossa boost) is evaluated for severe growth deceleration. His height velocity has dropped from the 50th percentile to 1.8 cm/year (<3rd percentile). He is in complete oncologic remission. Bone age is delayed by 2.5 years. Two provocative growth hormone stimulation tests show peak GH levels of 2.1 ng/mL and 1.8 ng/mL, with low serum IGF-1. What is the most appropriate next step in management?

A. Initiate recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) therapy at 0.16-0.24 mg/kg/week with regular clinical and oncologic monitoring
B. Withhold growth hormone therapy permanently due to an unacceptably high risk of medulloblastoma recurrence
C. Initiate high-dose depot testosterone enanthate 200 mg monthly to promote linear growth
D. Perform immediate emergent craniotomy and repeat posterior fossa resection

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Initiate recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) therapy at 0.16-0.24 mg/kg/week with regular clinical and oncologic monitoring

Growth hormone deficiency is the most common and earliest endocrine complication of cranial irradiation (occurring at doses >=18 Gy). Landmark data from the Childhood Cancer Survivor Study (CCSS) have conclusively proven that rhGH replacement does NOT increase the risk of primary central nervous system tumor recurrence or leukemia relapse in disease-free survivors. Because he has been in remission for >1 year, initiating rhGH therapy is standard care to restore linear growth.

## Authored MCQ

A 6-year-old girl who received 24 Gy cranial irradiation for acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) 2 years ago presents with rapid linear growth acceleration and bilateral breast enlargement (Tanner stage 3). Bone age is advanced by 2 years. A GnRH stimulation test reveals a peak stimulated LH of 8.4 IU/L. Cranial MRI shows no leukemic relapse or mass. What is the mechanism of precocious puberty in this patient?

A. Radiation-induced damage to cortical and hypothalamic inhibitory pathways, disinhibiting the GnRH pulse generator
B. Ectopic LH secretion by residual leukemic blast cells
C. Primary ovarian granulosa cell tumor induced by chemotherapy
D. Peripheral aromatization of adrenal androgens

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Radiation-induced damage to cortical and hypothalamic inhibitory pathways, disinhibiting the GnRH pulse generator

Low-to-moderate dose cranial irradiation (18-24 Gy), particularly in girls treated at a young age (<8 years), frequently causes central precocious puberty. Radiation damages the higher cortical and hypothalamic neural pathways that normally exert tonic inhibitory restraint over the GnRH pulse generator, resulting in premature disinhibition of GnRH pulsatility, true central precocious puberty, accelerated bone age, and compromised adult height.

## Authored MCQ

A 17-year-old male survivor of adolescent Hodgkin lymphoma who received cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and procarbazine (cumulative Cyclophosphamide Equivalent Dose [CED] 9500 mg/m2) presents for late-effects evaluation. Physical examination reveals normal virilization (Tanner stage 5 pubic hair, adult phallus), but bilateral soft, small testes measuring 6 mL each. Laboratory evaluation reveals: serum testosterone 540 ng/dL (normal male), LH 4.8 IU/L (normal), and FSH 32 IU/L (markedly elevated). Semen analysis confirms azoospermia. How should these findings be explained?

A. Selective alkylator-induced germ cell destruction (azoospermia with high FSH) with spared, functioning Leydig cells (normal testosterone and LH)
B. Secondary hypogonadotropic hypogonadism due to hypothalamic radiation damage
C. Complete primary hypogonadism requiring immediate testosterone replacement therapy
D. Retrograde ejaculation caused by vincristine autonomic neuropathy

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Selective alkylator-induced germ cell destruction (azoospermia with high FSH) with spared, functioning Leydig cells (normal testosterone and LH)

High cumulative doses of alkylating chemotherapy (CED >=8000 mg/m2) exert selective cytotoxicity against rapidly dividing testicular germ cells (spermatogonia), causing permanent azoospermia and loss of inhibin B feedback, which leads to markedly elevated FSH. In contrast, non-dividing Leydig cells are highly resistant to alkylating agents, maintaining normal testosterone production, normal LH levels, and normal secondary sexual virilization.

## Authored MCQ

A 22-year-old female who was treated at age 7 with 35 Gy mantle field radiation for Hodgkin lymphoma presents for routine survivor surveillance. What is the most critical lifelong annual screening recommendation for her thyroid health?

A. Annual neck palpation and clinical screening for primary hypothyroidism and secondary thyroid carcinoma (papillary thyroid carcinoma)
B. Prophylactic total thyroidectomy before age 25
C. Routine annual 131I radioactive iodine diagnostic whole-body scans
D. Empirical levothyroxine therapy to suppress TSH to undetectable levels

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Annual neck palpation and clinical screening for primary hypothyroidism and secondary thyroid carcinoma (papillary thyroid carcinoma)

Survivors of childhood cancer exposed to direct neck or mantle radiation (>=10-20 Gy) have a 20-50% lifetime risk of primary hypothyroidism and an exponentially increased risk of radiation-induced differentiated thyroid carcinoma (predominantly papillary thyroid carcinoma), which peaks 15-25 years post-exposure. Children's Oncology Group (COG) guidelines mandate lifelong annual clinical thyroid examination and serum TSH monitoring.

## Authored MCQ

A 16-year-old male who received 54 Gy cranial radiation for a hypothalamic astrocytoma 8 years ago presents to urgent care with severe lethargy, orthostatic dizziness, and nausea during a viral illness. Blood pressure is 82/46 mmHg. Serum sodium is 126 mEq/L and potassium is 4.2 mEq/L. Morning cortisol drawn in the clinic is 1.8 mcg/dL with plasma ACTH 8 pg/mL (low). What is the diagnosis and urgent therapy?

A. Late-onset radiation-induced central adrenal insufficiency; immediate IV normal saline resuscitation and IV hydrocortisone bolus
B. Autoimmune Addison disease; fludrocortisone monotherapy
C. Dehydration alone; oral rehydration solution
D. Radiation-induced nephrogenic diabetes insipidus; desmopressin

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Late-onset radiation-induced central adrenal insufficiency; immediate IV normal saline resuscitation and IV hydrocortisone bolus

High-dose cranial radiation (>=40-50 Gy) causes late-onset central (secondary) adrenal insufficiency that can manifest 5 to 15+ years after radiotherapy. Presenting with low cortisol (1.8 mcg/dL) and inappropriately low ACTH (8 pg/mL) in the setting of infection and hypotension confirms acute adrenal crisis secondary to radiation-induced ACTH deficiency. Immediate management requires IV isotonic saline and an emergency IV hydrocortisone bolus (50-100 mg/m2).
