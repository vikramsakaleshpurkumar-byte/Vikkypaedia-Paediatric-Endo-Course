## Authored MCQ

A 6-year-old girl is evaluated for bilateral breast development (Tanner stage 3) and a growth spurt over the past 8 months. Height is at the 97th percentile (previously 50th percentile). Left hand radiograph reveals a bone age of 8.5 years. A GnRH stimulation test is performed: baseline LH is 0.8 IU/L, and peak stimulated LH at 30 minutes is 9.4 IU/L with peak FSH 7.2 IU/L. Contrast-enhanced brain MRI of the hypothalamic-pituitary region is normal. What is the most appropriate next step in management?

A. Initiate long-acting GnRH agonist therapy (e.g., depot leuprolide or histrelin implant)
B. Perform bilateral laparoscopic oophorectomy
C. Prescribe oral spironolactone and anastrozole
D. Observation without treatment until menarche occurs

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Initiate long-acting GnRH agonist therapy (e.g., depot leuprolide or histrelin implant)

This patient has idiopathic central precocious puberty (CPP), documented by early thelarche (<8 years), accelerated growth velocity, advanced bone age, and a robust pubertal peak LH >5.0 IU/L on GnRH stimulation test with a normal brain MRI. In girls under 7-8 years with rapidly progressive CPP and compromised predicted adult height, long-acting GnRH agonists (e.g., depot leuprolide 11.25 mg every 3 months or histrelin implant) are the first-line treatment to preserve final adult height and arrest sexual maturation.

## Authored MCQ

A 5-year-old boy presents with pubic hair development, penile enlargement, and linear growth acceleration. On physical examination, his phallus is 8.5 cm in length, pubic hair is Tanner stage 3, and his testes are symmetric with a volume of 2.0 mL bilaterally (prepubertal). Morning laboratory evaluation reveals serum total testosterone 180 ng/dL (elevated for age), and basal and GnRH-stimulated LH levels are <0.1 IU/L. What is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Non-salt-wasting congenital adrenal hyperplasia (21-hydroxylase deficiency)
B. Idiopathic central precocious puberty
C. Hypothalamic hamartoma
D. Leydig cell testicular adenoma

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Non-salt-wasting congenital adrenal hyperplasia (21-hydroxylase deficiency)

This boy exhibits peripheral precocious puberty (isosexual pseudoprecocity), characterized by marked virilization (phallic growth, pubic hair, high testosterone) with completely prepubertal testes (<4 mL bilaterally) and suppressed gonadotropins (LH <0.1 IU/L). In boys, the most common cause of peripheral precocity with small prepubertal testes is congenital adrenal hyperplasia (simple virilizing 21-hydroxylase deficiency), where adrenal androgens virilize the external genitalia while suppressing the pituitary HPG axis.

## Authored MCQ

A 3-year-old male presents with aggressive behavior, accelerated linear growth, phallic enlargement, and bilateral symmetric testicular enlargement (testicular volume 6-8 mL bilaterally). Total testosterone is 350 ng/dL. A GnRH stimulation test shows baseline and peak LH <0.1 IU/L. Cranial MRI is completely normal. Genetic testing reveals an activating heterozygous missense mutation in the LHCGR gene. What is the diagnosis?

A. Testotoxicosis (familial male-limited precocious puberty)
B. Complete androgen insensitivity syndrome
C. McCune-Albright syndrome
D. Hypothalamic hamartoma

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Testotoxicosis (familial male-limited precocious puberty)

Testotoxicosis (familial male-limited precocious puberty) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by activating mutations in LHCGR (luteinizing hormone / choriogonadotropin receptor). The constitutively active LH receptor autonomously stimulates Leydig cell cAMP production, causing bilateral testicular enlargement (6-8 mL) and adult-level testosterone secretion in early childhood despite prepubertal/suppressed pituitary LH. Females carrying the mutation are unaffected because ovarian steroidogenesis requires both LH and FSH.

## Authored MCQ

A 4-year-old girl presents with recurrent episodes of vaginal bleeding without breast enlargement or pubic hair. Physical examination reveals two large light-brown macules on her lower back and buttocks with jagged, irregular borders that do not cross the midline. Skeletal radiographs demonstrate expansile radiolucent lesions with ground-glass appearance in the left femur and pelvis. What is the diagnosis and molecular etiology?

A. McCune-Albright syndrome due to postzygotic activating somatic mutation in GNAS
B. Neurofibromatosis type 1 due to NF1 germline mutation
C. Central precocious puberty due to MKRN3 mutation
D. Peutz-Jeghers syndrome due to STK11 mutation

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

McCune-Albright syndrome due to postzygotic activating somatic mutation in GNAS

McCune-Albright syndrome (MAS) is defined by the classical triad of: (1) peripheral precocious puberty with recurrent large autonomous ovarian cysts and vaginal bleeding; (2) large café-au-lait pigmentation with jagged, irregular 'coast of Maine' borders that typically respect the midline; and (3) polyostotic fibrous dysplasia (ground-glass bone lesions). MAS is caused by a postzygotic mosaic activating somatic mutation in GNAS (encoding Gs-alpha, typically at Arg201), constitutively activating adenylyl cyclase.

## Authored MCQ

A 15-month-old healthy, thriving girl is noted to have bilateral palpable breast tissue (Tanner stage 2) on routine examination. There is no pubic or axillary hair. Her growth velocity is tracking steadily along the 50th percentile, and left hand radiograph confirms that her bone age is identical to chronological age (15 months). Pelvic ultrasound shows prepubertal uterine length (2.2 cm) without endometrial thickening and normal infantile ovaries. What is the recommended management?

A. Observation and reassurance without pharmacological intervention (benign premature thelarche)
B. Initiate depot leuprolide 7.5 mg IM monthly
C. Order emergent head MRI to rule out a hypothalamic tumor
D. Perform bilateral breast needle aspiration biopsy

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Observation and reassurance without pharmacological intervention (benign premature thelarche)

This infant has isolated premature thelarche, a common benign pubertal variant that occurs in girls under 2 years of age. It represents transient sensitivity of breast tissue to maternal/neonatal estrogens or transient incomplete activation of the HPG axis with isolated FSH secretion. It is characterized by normal growth velocity, normal bone age, prepubertal pelvic ultrasound, and spontaneous regression or stabilization over 1-2 years. No medical intervention or brain MRI is indicated.
