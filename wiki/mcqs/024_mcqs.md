## Authored MCQ

A 14.5-year-old boy is brought to the endocrine clinic by his parents because he has not shown any signs of puberty. On examination, he is prepubertal (Tanner stage 1: no pubic hair, bilateral testicular volume 2.5 mL). His height is at the 5th percentile, but his calculated midparental target height is at the 50th percentile. His father underwent a late growth spurt at age 16. Left hand radiograph reveals a bone age of 11.5 years (delayed by 3 years). Serum LH is 0.4 IU/L, FSH is 0.8 IU/L, and morning total testosterone is 15 ng/dL. What is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Constitutional delay of growth and puberty (CDGP)
B. Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY)
C. Complete androgen insensitivity syndrome
D. Craniopharyngioma with panhypopituitarism

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Constitutional delay of growth and puberty (CDGP)

This boy exhibits the classic hallmark profile of constitutional delay of growth and puberty (CDGP): male sex, lack of pubertal onset by age 14 (testicular volume <4 mL), positive paternal history of delayed pubertal timing ('late bloomer'), delayed bone age (11.5 years) that matches his height age, and normal prepubertal gonadotropins and testosterone. When his bone age advances to 12-13 years, spontaneous puberty will occur.

## Authored MCQ

A 16-year-old phenotypic female presents for evaluation of primary amenorrhea and lack of pubertal development. Physical examination reveals Tanner stage 1 breasts, Tanner stage 1 pubic hair, a high-arched palate, low posterior hairline, and wide-spaced nipples. Her height is well below the 3rd percentile. Serum FSH is markedly elevated at 88 IU/L and LH is 34 IU/L. Pelvic ultrasound reveals a small prepubertal uterus and bilateral streak ovaries. What diagnostic test should be ordered next?

A. Peripheral blood karyotype analysis
B. Intravenous GnRH stimulation test
C. Head MRI with dedicated pituitary cuts
D. 24-hour urinary free cortisol

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Peripheral blood karyotype analysis

The clinical constellation of short stature, primary amenorrhea, absent breast development, webbed neck/low hairline, and hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (elevated FSH/LH with streak ovaries) is pathognomonic for Turner syndrome. A peripheral blood karyotype analysis (with count of at least 30-50 metaphase cells to rule out mosaicism and Y-chromosome material) is the mandatory next diagnostic test.

## Authored MCQ

A 15-year-old boy presents with complete absence of pubertal development (testicular volume 2 mL bilaterally). On detailed review of systems, he reports that he has never been able to smell coffee, smoke, or perfume. Serum LH and FSH are low-normal, and morning testosterone is <10 ng/dL. Cranial MRI reveals aplasia of the olfactory bulbs and absent olfactory sulci. What is the diagnosis?

A. Kallmann syndrome
B. Klinefelter syndrome
C. Prader-Willi syndrome
D. Vanishing testes syndrome

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Kallmann syndrome

Kallmann syndrome is congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism paired with anosmia or hyposmia. It is caused by mutations in genes such as ANOS1 (KAL1), FGFR1, or PROKR2 that disrupt the embryonic co-migration of GnRH neuroendocrine cells and olfactory axons from the olfactory placode into the forebrain. Absent olfactory bulbs/sulci on MRI confirms the diagnosis.

## Authored MCQ

A 13-year-old girl with confirmed Turner syndrome (45,X) and complete ovarian failure is ready to begin pubertal induction. What is the recommended strategy for introducing estrogen and progesterone?

A. Initiate very low-dose transdermal 17-beta estradiol monotherapy, gradually escalating over 2-3 years, and delay adding cyclic progesterone for at least 18-24 months
B. Initiate combination oral contraceptive pills containing 35 mcg ethinyl estradiol and norgestimate immediately
C. Administer high-dose oral conjugated estrogens with daily medroxyprogesterone from day 1
D. Withhold estrogen therapy until chronological age 18 years to maximize final adult height

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Initiate very low-dose transdermal 17-beta estradiol monotherapy, gradually escalating over 2-3 years, and delay adding cyclic progesterone for at least 18-24 months

Consensus guidelines for Turner syndrome recommend initiating pubertal induction at age 11-12 years with very low-dose 17-beta estradiol (preferably transdermal patches at 6.25-12.5 mcg/day) to mimic normal physiological puberty. Estrogen is escalated gradually over 2 to 3 years to optimize breast contour, alveolar development, and uterine dimensions. Cyclic progesterone is delayed for 18-24 months (or until breakthrough bleeding occurs) to avoid tubular breast deformity and premature cessation of ductal proliferation.

## Authored MCQ

A 15-year-old male with constitutional delay of growth and puberty (CDGP) is experiencing severe anxiety, social withdrawal, and school bullying due to his short stature and lack of sexual development. His bone age is 12 years and testicular volume is 3 mL bilaterally. What is the most appropriate medical management?

A. Reassurance alone with strict refusal of any pharmacological intervention
B. A short 3- to 6-month course of low-dose depot testosterone enanthate (25-50 mg IM once monthly)
C. Full adult replacement testosterone enanthate (200 mg IM every 2 weeks)
D. Subcutaneous recombinant growth hormone therapy at supraphysiological doses

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

A short 3- to 6-month course of low-dose depot testosterone enanthate (25-50 mg IM once monthly)

In adolescent boys with CDGP >=14 years of age with significant psychological distress, a short course of low-dose depot testosterone (25-50 mg IM monthly for 3-6 months) is the standard of care. This regimen accelerates linear growth, induces virilization (pubic hair, phallic enlargement), and promotes psychological well-being without causing excessive bone age advancement or compromising adult height. It frequently stimulates the endogenous hypothalamic GnRH pulse generator.
