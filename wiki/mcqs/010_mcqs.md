## Authored MCQ

An 8-year-old boy presents to the emergency department with severe vomiting, lethargy, and dehydration. Blood pressure is 74/42 mmHg and heart rate is 144 bpm. Physical exam reveals marked hyperpigmentation of the gingival mucosa and knuckles. Laboratory analysis shows sodium 118 mEq/L, potassium 6.8 mEq/L, and blood glucose 42 mg/dL. What is the immediate life-saving treatment?

A. Intravenous bolus of 0.9% normal saline (20 mL/kg) with 5% dextrose and immediate IV hydrocortisone 50 mg
B. Continuous intravenous insulin infusion with 50% dextrose to treat hyperkalemia
C. Immediate oral fludrocortisone 0.1 mg and oral rehydration solution
D. Intravenous hypertonic 3% sodium chloride bolus without glucocorticoids

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Intravenous bolus of 0.9% normal saline (20 mL/kg) with 5% dextrose and immediate IV hydrocortisone 50 mg

This patient is in acute primary adrenal crisis with hypotensive shock, severe hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, and hypoglycemia. Immediate resuscitation with isotonic saline/dextrose boluses and parenteral hydrocortisone is vital.

## Authored MCQ

A 7-year-old previously healthy boy develops progressive fatigue, declining school performance, and skin bronzing over 6 months. Neurological exam reveals hyperreflexia and mild visual tracking impairment. Plasma ACTH is 840 pg/mL with morning cortisol 2.1 mcg/dL. Plasma renin activity is elevated. What diagnostic test should be prioritized to identify the underlying etiology?

A. Plasma very long-chain fatty acid (VLCFA) analysis and brain MRI
B. Sweat chloride test to rule out cystic fibrosis
C. Serum anti-streptolysin O titer and complement levels
D. Gastric emptying scintigraphy and upper endoscopy

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Plasma very long-chain fatty acid (VLCFA) analysis and brain MRI

In young boys presenting with primary adrenal insufficiency (elevated ACTH, low cortisol) and subtle neurocognitive changes, X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy (ABCD1 mutation) must be excluded by measuring plasma very long-chain fatty acids (VLCFA) and brain MRI to detect cerebral demyelination.

## Authored MCQ

A 12-year-old girl with autoimmune Addison disease (primary adrenocortical failure) on maintenance hydrocortisone (9 mg/m2/day) and fludrocortisone (0.1 mg daily) develops acute streptococcal pharyngitis with fever of 39.4°C (103°F). What instruction must be given to her parents regarding steroid dosing?

A. Triple her oral hydrocortisone dose (25–30 mg/m2/day) divided every 8 hours until fever resolves, keeping fludrocortisone unchanged
B. Discontinue hydrocortisone completely to allow the immune system to fight the bacterial infection
C. Increase fludrocortisone to 1.0 mg daily and reduce hydrocortisone to half dose
D. Administer oral dexamethasone 4 mg once daily without taking any fluids

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Triple her oral hydrocortisone dose (25–30 mg/m2/day) divided every 8 hours until fever resolves, keeping fludrocortisone unchanged

During febrile illness (>38.5°C), endogenous cortisol secretion in healthy individuals triples. Patients with adrenal insufficiency require 'stress dosing' (doubling or tripling the maintenance oral hydrocortisone dose) to prevent adrenal crisis. Fludrocortisone dosing remains unchanged.

## Authored MCQ

A 15-year-old boy with severe Crohn disease has been treated with oral prednisone (40 mg daily) for 6 months. His gastroenterologist abruptly stops the prednisone after achieving mucosal healing. Five days later, the boy presents with severe fatigue, dizziness, nausea, and a blood pressure of 82/48 mmHg. Laboratory testing shows sodium 132 mEq/L, potassium 4.2 mEq/L, and glucose 64 mg/dL. What is the diagnosis?

A. Tertiary (iatrogenic) adrenal insufficiency due to prolonged hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis suppression
B. Acute primary autoimmune adrenalitis with destruction of all three adrenocortical zones
C. Acute primary mineralocorticoid deficiency secondary to renal tubular injury
D. Cushing disease relapse secondary to an ACTH-secreting pituitary macroadenoma

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Tertiary (iatrogenic) adrenal insufficiency due to prolonged hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis suppression

Prolonged exogenous supraphysiological glucocorticoid therapy suppresses hypothalamic CRH and pituitary ACTH secretion, resulting in adrenocortical atrophy. Abrupt cessation unmasks central/tertiary adrenal insufficiency, characterized by normal potassium (intact mineralocorticoid axis) but symptomatic glucocorticoid deficiency.

## Authored MCQ

A 6-year-old girl presents with recurrent episodes of hypoglycemia, fatigue, and alacrima (inability to produce tears). Physical examination also reveals mild difficulty swallowing (achalasia). Laboratory testing confirms primary adrenocortical failure with ACTH 650 pg/mL and cortisol 1.8 mcg/dL. What rare autosomal recessive syndrome is defined by this constellation of findings?

A. Triple A syndrome (Allgrove syndrome, caused by AAAS mutations)
B. Autoimmune Polyendocrine Syndrome type 1 (APS-1, caused by AIRE mutations)
C. Werdnig-Hoffmann spinal muscular atrophy
D. Congenital adrenal hyperplasia due to 11-beta-hydroxylase deficiency

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Triple A syndrome (Allgrove syndrome, caused by AAAS mutations)

Triple A syndrome (Allgrove syndrome) is an autosomal recessive condition caused by mutations in the AAAS gene (encoding the nuclear pore protein aladin), characterized by the triad of Adrenal insufficiency, Achalasia, and Alacrima.
