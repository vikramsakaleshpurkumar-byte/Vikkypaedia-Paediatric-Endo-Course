## Authored MCQ

A 14-year-old Hispanic male with a BMI of 34 kg/m2 (98th percentile) presents for a routine sports physical. Examination reveals prominent velvety hyperpigmented plaques on the back of his neck and axillae (acanthosis nigricans). Random blood glucose is 215 mg/dL. Confirmatory testing demonstrates fasting blood glucose 152 mg/dL and HbA1c 7.8%. Urinalysis shows glucosuria without ketones. Islet autoantibodies (GAD65, IA-2, ZnT8) are negative. What is the most appropriate initial pharmacological treatment?

A. Oral metformin initiated at 500 mg once daily, titrated up to 2000 mg daily in divided doses, plus lifestyle intervention
B. Immediate initiation of basal-bolus insulin at 1.0 units/kg/day
C. Sulfonylurea monotherapy with glipizide 10 mg daily
D. Lifestyle modification alone with no medication for 12 months

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Oral metformin initiated at 500 mg once daily, titrated up to 2000 mg daily in divided doses, plus lifestyle intervention

In youth diagnosed with antibody-negative type 2 diabetes who are metabolically stable (HbA1c <8.5%, blood glucose <250 mg/dL, no ketosis/acidosis), oral metformin monotherapy (titrated from 500 mg daily up to 1000 mg twice daily) combined with diabetes self-management education and lifestyle modification is the recommended first-line therapy according to ADA and ISPAD guidelines.

## Authored MCQ

A 15-year-old obese female presents with polyuria, polydipsia, and fatigue. Serum glucose is 340 mg/dL and HbA1c is 10.4%. Serum electrolytes: sodium 136 mEq/L, potassium 4.2 mEq/L, bicarbonate 22 mEq/L, and venous pH 7.36. Urinalysis shows 4+ glucose and trace ketones. What is the recommended initial medical management?

A. Initiation of once-daily subcutaneous basal insulin (0.25-0.5 units/kg/day) combined with oral metformin
B. Intravenous insulin infusion at 0.1 units/kg/hour according to DKA protocol
C. Metformin monotherapy 500 mg daily without insulin
D. Immediate bariatric Roux-en-Y gastric bypass surgery

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Initiation of once-daily subcutaneous basal insulin (0.25-0.5 units/kg/day) combined with oral metformin

In youth with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes presenting with marked hyperglycemia (blood glucose >=250 mg/dL and/or HbA1c >=8.5%) WITHOUT ketoacidosis (normal pH and bicarbonate), the recommended management is initiation of once-daily subcutaneous basal insulin (0.25-0.5 units/kg/day) combined with oral metformin. Once glucotoxicity is relieved and glycemic stability is attained, insulin can frequently be tapered.

## Authored MCQ

A 16-year-old African American male with newly discovered T2D presents in a somnolent, severely dehydrated state. Laboratory findings demonstrate: serum glucose 1050 mg/dL, venous pH 7.32, serum bicarbonate 18 mmol/L, serum sodium 152 mEq/L, potassium 5.6 mEq/L, and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) 54 mg/dL. Effective serum osmolality is calculated at 345 mOsm/kg. Urine dipstick shows trace ketones. What is the diagnosis, and what is the primary initial resuscitation priority?

A. Hyperglycemic Hyperosmolar State (HHS); vigorous isotonic fluid resuscitation with 0.9% normal saline
B. Severe diabetic ketoacidosis; immediate IV insulin bolus followed by high-dose infusion
C. Central diabetes insipidus; intravenous desmopressin administration
D. Lactic acidosis secondary to metformin toxicity; emergent hemodialysis

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Hyperglycemic Hyperosmolar State (HHS); vigorous isotonic fluid resuscitation with 0.9% normal saline

This patient has Hyperglycemic Hyperosmolar State (HHS), characterized by extreme hyperglycemia (>600 mg/dL), severe hyperosmolality (>320 mOsm/kg), and altered sensorium in the absence of significant ketoacidosis (pH >7.30, bicarb >15). HHS carries a 10-30% mortality in youth. The primary resuscitation priority is urgent, aggressive expansion of the intravascular volume using isotonic fluids (0.9% saline 20 mL/kg boluses) to restore tissue perfusion before starting low-dose insulin.

## Authored MCQ

A 15-year-old female with youth-onset T2D on metformin 2000 mg daily has a repeat HbA1c of 8.2% after 6 months. She has a BMI of 35 kg/m2. She expresses desire to improve glycemic control and lose weight without daily fingerstick insulin adjustments. Which class of medication is FDA-approved for youth aged >=10 years and addresses both glycemic control and weight reduction?

A. GLP-1 receptor agonist (e.g., once-weekly subcutaneous dulaglutide or once-daily liraglutide)
B. Sulfonylurea (e.g., glimepiride)
C. Thiazolidinedione (e.g., pioglitazone)
D. Alpha-glucosidase inhibitor (e.g., acarbose)

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

GLP-1 receptor agonist (e.g., once-weekly subcutaneous dulaglutide or once-daily liraglutide)

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (e.g., once-daily liraglutide or once-weekly dulaglutide/exenatide) are FDA-approved as add-on therapy for youth aged >=10 years with type 2 diabetes. GLP-1 receptor agonists stimulate glucose-dependent insulin secretion, suppress inappropriate glucagon release, delay gastric emptying, and promote central satiety, leading to significant HbA1c reductions and clinically meaningful weight loss.

## Authored MCQ

Unlike children with type 1 diabetes where microvascular screening begins after 2 to 5 years, why must screening for diabetic kidney disease (urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio) and retinopathy begin at the time of initial diagnosis in youth with type 2 diabetes?

A. Youth with type 2 diabetes frequently have subclinical disease for months to years prior to diagnosis, and microvascular complications progress far more rapidly
B. Screening tests are only valid during the first month of diabetes diagnosis
C. Type 2 diabetes damages renal glomeruli through immune-complex glomerulonephritis
D. Retinal examination is required to rule out retinitis pigmentosa

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Youth with type 2 diabetes frequently have subclinical disease for months to years prior to diagnosis, and microvascular complications progress far more rapidly

In youth-onset type 2 diabetes, the preceding asymptomatic period of insulin resistance and impaired glucose tolerance often persists for years before clinical diagnosis. Studies (such as the TODAY study) have shown that up to 10-20% of youth already have microalbuminuria or early diabetic retinopathy at the time of initial diagnosis, and microvascular complications progress at an accelerated rate compared to type 1 diabetes. Therefore, immediate baseline screening is mandatory.
