## Authored MCQ

An 8-year-old boy presents with tetany and generalized seizures. Serum calcium is 6.1 mg/dL, phosphate is 8.4 mg/dL, and intact PTH is 4 pg/mL (inappropriately low). His medical history is notable for recurrent, treatment-resistant oral and esophageal thrush since age 18 months, as well as pitted transverse grooves across his permanent incisors. What is the underlying genetic defect?

A. Autosomal recessive mutation in the AIRE gene on chromosome 21q22.3
B. Hemizygous mutation in the FOXP3 gene on chromosome Xp11.23
C. Heterozygous activating mutation in the CaSR gene
D. Autosomal dominant mutation in the MEN1 gene

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Autosomal recessive mutation in the AIRE gene on chromosome 21q22.3

This patient meets criteria for Autoimmune Polyglandular Syndrome Type 1 (APS-1 / APECED), having chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis (CMC), autoimmune hypoparathyroidism, and dental enamel hypoplasia. APS-1 is caused by autosomal recessive mutations in the AIRE (autoimmune regulator) gene on chromosome 21q22.3, which disrupts thymic negative selection of autoreactive T-cell clones.

## Authored MCQ

A 16-year-old girl with established type 1 diabetes presents with progressive fatigue, postural dizziness, weight loss, and darkening of her skin across her palmar creases and gums. Laboratory testing demonstrates serum sodium 128 mEq/L, potassium 5.6 mEq/L, TSH 18.4 mIU/L, free T4 0.6 ng/dL, and morning baseline cortisol 2.2 mcg/dL with plasma ACTH 850 pg/mL. Thyroid antibodies (anti-TPO) and 21-hydroxylase antibodies are strongly positive. What autoimmune syndrome is present?

A. Autoimmune Polyglandular Syndrome Type 2 (APS-2 / Schmidt syndrome)
B. Autoimmune Polyglandular Syndrome Type 1 (APECED)
C. McCune-Albright syndrome
D. Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia Type 1

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Autoimmune Polyglandular Syndrome Type 2 (APS-2 / Schmidt syndrome)

Autoimmune Polyglandular Syndrome Type 2 (APS-2 / Schmidt syndrome) is characterized by the mandatory presence of primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison disease; elevated ACTH, low cortisol, hyperkalemia, hyperpigmentation) in combination with autoimmune thyroid disease (elevated TSH, low FT4) and/or type 1 diabetes. Chronic candidiasis and hypoparathyroidism are absent, distinguishing it from APS-1.

## Authored MCQ

A 3-week-old male infant presents with failure to thrive, severe exfoliative eczematous dermatitis, intractable voluminous watery diarrhea, and a random blood glucose of 480 mg/dL with glucosuria. Flow cytometry demonstrates complete absence of CD4+CD25+FoxP3+ regulatory T cells. What is the diagnosis and definitive curative treatment?

A. IPEX syndrome; allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)
B. APS-1; high-dose oral fluconazole and calcium carbonate
C. Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID); enzyme replacement therapy with pegademase
D. Cystic fibrosis; pancreatic enzyme replacement

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

IPEX syndrome; allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)

IPEX (Immunodysregulation Polyendocrinopathy Enteropathy X-linked) syndrome is caused by mutations in FOXP3 on chromosome Xp11.23, leading to absence or functional failure of CD4+CD25+ regulatory T cells. Male infants present in the first weeks of life with the lethal triad of intractable autoimmune enteropathy (secretory diarrhea), neonatal type 1 diabetes, and severe dermatitis/eczema. Without allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), IPEX is fatal in early childhood.

## Authored MCQ

A 10-year-old girl with confirmed Autoimmune Polyglandular Syndrome Type 1 (APS-1) has hypoparathyroidism controlled on calcitriol. She is being monitored for development of additional autoimmune endocrinopathies. What is the most common subsequent endocrine component that typically develops between ages 10 and 15 years?

A. Primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison disease)
B. Central diabetes insipidus
C. Primary hyperparathyroidism
D. Medullary thyroid carcinoma

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison disease)

In APS-1, primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison disease) is the third cardinal component of the diagnostic triad, typically developing between 10 and 15 years of age (following candidiasis in infancy and hypoparathyroidism in early childhood). Annual surveillance with plasma renin activity, morning ACTH, and electrolytes, or testing for 21-hydroxylase antibodies, is mandatory to detect subclinical adrenal insufficiency before fatal crisis occurs.

## Authored MCQ

Why must oral hydrocortisone always be initiated prior to or concurrently with levothyroxine in a patient diagnosed with concurrent Addison disease and autoimmune hypothyroidism?

A. Levothyroxine accelerates hepatic clearance and metabolic turnover of cortisol, which will precipitate acute adrenal crisis in a cortisol-deficient patient
B. Hydrocortisone prevents thyroid hormone binding to plasma albumin
C. Levothyroxine causes severe hyperkalemia if given alone
D. Hydrocortisone inhibits renal excretion of levothyroxine

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Levothyroxine accelerates hepatic clearance and metabolic turnover of cortisol, which will precipitate acute adrenal crisis in a cortisol-deficient patient

Thyroid hormone stimulates the hepatic expression of corticosteroid-metabolizing enzymes (such as 11-beta-HSD and 5-alpha/beta-reductases), markedly accelerating the clearance and degradation of endogenous cortisol. In a patient with underlying, untreated adrenal insufficiency, administering levothyroxine alone rapidly depletes residual circulating cortisol, precipitating an acute, life-threatening adrenal crisis with shock and vascular collapse.
