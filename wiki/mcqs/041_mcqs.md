## Authored MCQ

A 5-year-old girl with meningococcal purpura fulminans presents with refractory septic shock. She has received 60 mL/kg of isotonic crystalloids, and despite continuous infusions of norepinephrine (0.4 mcg/kg/min) and epinephrine (0.3 mcg/kg/min), her mean arterial pressure remains 15 mmHg below the 5th percentile for age, with cold extremities and oliguria. Blood gas shows severe metabolic acidosis. What adjunctive endocrine pharmacotherapy is indicated?

A. Intravenous hydrocortisone administered as a 50 mg/m2 (or 2 mg/kg) bolus followed by 50-100 mg/m2/day continuous infusion
B. Intravenous levothyroxine 2 mcg/kg daily
C. Subcutaneous recombinant human growth hormone 0.05 mg/kg daily
D. High-dose intravenous sodium bicarbonate infusion

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Intravenous hydrocortisone administered as a 50 mg/m2 (or 2 mg/kg) bolus followed by 50-100 mg/m2/day continuous infusion

According to the Pediatric Surviving Sepsis Campaign and endocrine consensus guidelines, in children with catecholamine-resistant, fluid-refractory septic shock, Critical Illness-Related Corticosteroid Insufficiency (CIRCI) or acute adrenal hemorrhage (Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome) should be assumed. Empiric 'stress-dose' intravenous hydrocortisone (50 mg/m2 or 2 mg/kg bolus, followed by 50-100 mg/m2/day) restores vascular adrenergic receptor responsiveness, stabilizes capillary permeability, and improves shock reversal.

## Authored MCQ

A 10-year-old boy in the pediatric intensive care unit following severe polytrauma has persistent blood glucose levels ranging from 220 to 260 mg/dL. The team initiates a continuous intravenous regular insulin infusion. According to current pediatric critical care consensus guidelines (informed by trials such as the CHiP trial), what target blood glucose range should be maintained?

A. Moderate glycemic control: 140 to 180 mg/dL (7.8 to 10.0 mmol/L)
B. Strict normoglycemia: 80 to 110 mg/dL (4.4 to 6.1 mmol/L)
C. Permissive extreme hyperglycemia: 250 to 350 mg/dL
D. Mild hypoglycemia: 50 to 70 mg/dL

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Moderate glycemic control: 140 to 180 mg/dL (7.8 to 10.0 mmol/L)

Multicenter pediatric randomized trials (such as the Control of Hyperglycemia in Paediatric Intensive Care [CHiP] trial) demonstrated that aiming for strict normoglycemia (80-110 mg/dL) in critically ill children significantly increases the incidence of severe iatrogenic hypoglycemia (<40 mg/dL) without conferring survival or morbidity benefits. Current pediatric guidelines recommend moderate glycemic control targeting blood glucose between 140 and 180 mg/dL.

## Authored MCQ

Why is the standard 250 mcg cosyntropin stimulation test no longer routinely recommended to diagnose Critical Illness-Related Corticosteroid Insufficiency (CIRCI) in critically ill pediatric patients with septic shock?

A. Endogenous adrenal cortisol secretion is already maximally stimulated in severe stress, and fluctuations in corticosteroid-binding globulin (CBG) invalidate total cortisol measurements
B. Cosyntropin causes irreversible adrenal apoplexy in sepsis
C. Cosyntropin cannot cross the blood-brain barrier
D. Serum cortisol immunoassays cannot measure cortisol in the presence of antibiotics

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Endogenous adrenal cortisol secretion is already maximally stimulated in severe stress, and fluctuations in corticosteroid-binding globulin (CBG) invalidate total cortisol measurements

In severe septic shock, endogenous hypothalamic CRH and pituitary ACTH are already markedly elevated, driving maximal adrenal steroidogenesis. A supraphysiological dose of cosyntropin (250 mcg) cannot further stimulate maximally activated adrenal glands, leading to false-positive diagnoses of adrenal insufficiency. Furthermore, rapid capillary leakage and reduced hepatic CBG synthesis cause total cortisol measurements to fall sharply despite normal free, biologically active cortisol levels.

## Authored MCQ

A 12-year-old child with severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) requiring venovenous ECMO for 3 weeks demonstrates persistent non-thyroidal illness syndrome (TSH 0.3 mIU/L, free T4 0.5 ng/dL, free T3 1.2 pg/mL, elevated reverse T3). What does evidence from pediatric clinical trials recommend regarding thyroid hormone replacement in this setting?

A. Thyroid hormone replacement with T4 or T3 is not recommended because clinical trials show no benefit in survival or extubation rates
B. Intravenous levothyroxine bolus of 5 mcg/kg followed by daily maintenance is mandatory
C. Continuous intravenous liothyronine infusion should be administered to accelerate ECMO decannulation
D. Total thyroidectomy should be performed to eliminate reverse T3 production

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Thyroid hormone replacement with T4 or T3 is not recommended because clinical trials show no benefit in survival or extubation rates

Randomized controlled trials in pediatric critical care have consistently demonstrated that administering thyroid hormone (either T4 or T3) to patients with non-thyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS) does not improve survival, reduce inotropic dependence, or shorten ECMO/ventilator duration. Furthermore, exogenous thyroid hormone suppresses endogenous TSH recovery and accelerates tissue catabolism. Guidelines universally advise against thyroid hormone replacement in pediatric critical illness.

## Authored MCQ

What neuroendocrine alteration characterizes the prolonged, chronic phase of critical illness in a child hospitalized in the PICU for over 4 weeks?

A. Persistent central suppression and loss of pulsatile secretion of anterior pituitary hormones (ACTH, TSH, GH, and gonadotropins)
B. Sustained autonomous hypersecretion of growth hormone and prolactin causing gigantism
C. Exaggerated thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) secretion producing secondary hyperthyroidism
D. Continuous hyperaldosteronism with severe hypokalemia

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Persistent central suppression and loss of pulsatile secretion of anterior pituitary hormones (ACTH, TSH, GH, and gonadotropins)

While the acute phase of critical illness features elevated cortisol and GH with peripheral hormone resistance, the prolonged chronic phase (>2-4 weeks) is characterized by neuroendocrine exhaustion. The hypothalamus and anterior pituitary suffer persistent loss of pulsatile secretion of ACTH, TSH, GH, and LH/FSH, producing a state of multi-axis central hypopituitarism that contributes to prolonged catabolism, muscle wasting, and delayed recovery.
