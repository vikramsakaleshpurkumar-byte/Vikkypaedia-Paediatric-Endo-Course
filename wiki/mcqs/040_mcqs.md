## Authored MCQ

A 4-year-old boy with known congenital adrenal hyperplasia (21-hydroxylase deficiency) presents with persistent vomiting, lethargy, and vascular collapse following a gastrointestinal viral infection. Heart rate is 175 bpm, blood pressure is 64/38 mmHg, and peripheral pulses are thready. Point-of-care glucose is 36 mg/dL. What is the most critical immediate pharmacological intervention?

A. Intravenous bolus of 0.9% normal saline (20 mL/kg) with 10% dextrose, combined with an immediate IV hydrocortisone bolus of 50 mg
B. Continuous dopamine infusion at 10 mcg/kg/min
C. Oral fludrocortisone 0.1 mg crushed in water
D. Immediate cosyntropin stimulation test before administering steroids

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Intravenous bolus of 0.9% normal saline (20 mL/kg) with 10% dextrose, combined with an immediate IV hydrocortisone bolus of 50 mg

This child is in decompensated acute adrenal crisis with hypovolemic shock and hypoglycemia. The emergency management priorities are: (1) immediate restoration of intravascular volume with IV 0.9% normal saline (20 mL/kg bolus) containing dextrose (D5W or D10W) to correct hypoglycemia; and (2) immediate IV hydrocortisone bolus (50 mg for ages 3-12 years, or 50-100 mg/m2). Treatment must NEVER be delayed for laboratory tests or stimulation protocols.

## Authored MCQ

A 13-year-old girl with Graves' disease who abruptly stopped taking methimazole presents with high fever (40.2°C), severe agitation, delirium, marked tremor, resting tachycardia of 180 bpm, and profuse diarrhea. A diagnosis of thyroid storm is made. In what sequence should pharmacological therapies be administered?

A. Beta-blocker (propranolol) and Methimazole first, followed by Lugol's iodine at least 1 hour later, accompanied by IV Hydrocortisone
B. Lugol's iodine first, followed 1 hour later by Methimazole and Aspirin
C. High-dose Aspirin, followed by radioactive iodine ablation
D. Immediate total thyroidectomy under general anesthesia

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Beta-blocker (propranolol) and Methimazole first, followed by Lugol's iodine at least 1 hour later, accompanied by IV Hydrocortisone

The proper sequence of therapy in thyroid storm is critical: (1) Propranolol to control life-threatening adrenergic hyperactivity; (2) Methimazole to block new thyroid hormone synthesis; (3) Lugol's iodine or potassium iodide administered AT LEAST 1 HOUR AFTER methimazole to block hormone release (giving iodine before methimazole provides substrate for more hormone synthesis); (4) IV Hydrocortisone to block peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion and support adrenal reserve. Aspirin is strictly contraindicated.

## Authored MCQ

A 9-year-old boy in DKA receives IV fluid resuscitation and a continuous insulin infusion. Three hours into therapy, his blood glucose is 280 mg/dL, venous pH is 7.18, and bicarbonate is 11 mmol/L. His nurse notices that he has become increasingly somnolent, complains of a pounding headache, and his pulse has dropped from 105 bpm to 52 bpm. What is the immediate treatment of choice?

A. Administer 3% hypertonic saline (3-5 mL/kg IV over 10-15 minutes) or 20% mannitol immediately at the bedside
B. Stop all insulin and give 50% dextrose bolus
C. Administer IV sodium bicarbonate to accelerate acidosis correction
D. Transport the child to the MRI scanner for brain imaging

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Administer 3% hypertonic saline (3-5 mL/kg IV over 10-15 minutes) or 20% mannitol immediately at the bedside

Headache, lethargy, and bradycardia (part of the Cushing triad) represent impending brainstem herniation from acute DKA cerebral edema. Emergent hyperosmolar therapy with 3% hypertonic saline (3-5 mL/kg over 10-15 min) or 20% mannitol (0.5-1.0 g/kg over 20 min) must be administered immediately at the bedside without delay for neuroimaging.

## Authored MCQ

A 15-year-old girl with medullary thyroid cancer and extensive skeletal metastases develops acute hypercalcemic crisis with serum calcium 15.2 mg/dL (reference: 8.8-10.2 mg/dL), severe dehydration, and lethargy. ECG shows a shortened QTc interval. What is the initial, most essential resuscitation measure?

A. Intravenous 0.9% normal saline hyperhydration at 2-3 times maintenance volume
B. Immediate intravenous furosemide bolus before giving fluids
C. Oral calcitriol 1 mcg twice daily
D. Immediate hemodialysis against a high-calcium dialysate

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Intravenous 0.9% normal saline hyperhydration at 2-3 times maintenance volume

Severe hypercalcemia causes nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, vomiting, and profound volume depletion, which reduces glomerular filtration rate and impairs renal calcium clearance. The first and most critical step in management is vigorous intravascular volume expansion with intravenous 0.9% normal saline at 2-3 times maintenance, which restores renal perfusion and promotes proximal tubular calcium excretion. Loop diuretics (furosemide) are added only AFTER full intravascular volume repletion.

## Authored MCQ

A 6-year-old child presents to the emergency department with acute carpopedal spasm, stridor from laryngospasm, and a generalized seizure. Serum ionized calcium is 0.65 mmol/L (reference: 1.15-1.32 mmol/L). What is the appropriate intravenous emergency therapy?

A. 10% Calcium gluconate 1-2 mL/kg (100-200 mg/kg) IV infused slowly over 10-20 minutes with continuous ECG monitoring
B. 10% Calcium chloride 5 mL/kg rapid IV push via a scalp vein
C. Oral calcium carbonate 2000 mg chewed immediately
D. Subcutaneous teriparatide 20 mcg

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

10% Calcium gluconate 1-2 mL/kg (100-200 mg/kg) IV infused slowly over 10-20 minutes with continuous ECG monitoring

Acute symptomatic hypocalcemia with laryngospasm, tetany, or seizures requires immediate intravenous 10% calcium gluconate at 1-2 mL/kg (100-200 mg/kg; maximum 1-2 g or 10-20 mL). The infusion must be administered slowly over 10 to 20 minutes under continuous cardiac rhythm monitoring to avoid bradycardia or cardiac arrest. Calcium chloride carries high risk of tissue necrosis on extravasation and is avoided in peripheral lines.
