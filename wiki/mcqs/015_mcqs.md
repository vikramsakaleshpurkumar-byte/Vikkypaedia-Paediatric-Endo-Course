## Authored MCQ

A 13-year-old girl is evaluated for declining academic grades, emotional lability, heat intolerance, and resting palpitations. On examination, pulse is 118 bpm, blood pressure is 128/62 mmHg, and she has a smooth, non-tender, diffusely enlarged thyroid gland with an audible continuous bruit. Laboratory testing reveals TSH <0.01 mIU/L, free T4 3.8 ng/dL (reference: 0.9-1.8 ng/dL), and highly elevated thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulin (TSI). What is the most appropriate initial medical therapy?

A. Propylthiouracil 5 mg/kg/day divided every 8 hours
B. Methimazole 0.2-0.5 mg/kg/day once daily plus atenolol for symptom control
C. Immediate radioactive iodine (131I) ablation at 15 mCi
D. Lugol's strong iodine solution monotherapy

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Methimazole 0.2-0.5 mg/kg/day once daily plus atenolol for symptom control

Methimazole (MMI, 0.2-0.5 mg/kg/day, up to 1.0 mg/kg/day) is the only antithyroid drug recommended for pediatric Graves' disease. Propylthiouracil (PTU) is black-box contraindicated due to fulminant hepatic necrosis. Beta-blockers (e.g., atenolol 1-2 mg/kg/day) are added to control adrenergic hyperactivation (tachycardia, tremor). 131I is a definitive modality usually considered if medical therapy fails or causes toxic reactions.

## Authored MCQ

A 14-year-old male with Graves' disease has been taking methimazole 20 mg daily for 4 weeks. He presents to the urgent care clinic with a 2-day history of high fever (39.2°C), severe sore throat, and dysphagia. Examination shows an erythematous pharynx with tonsillar exudate. What is the most critical immediate clinical action?

A. Prescribe oral amoxicillin and continue methimazole unchanged
B. Immediately discontinue methimazole and obtain an emergent complete blood count with differential
C. Increase methimazole to 30 mg daily to treat an acute thyrotoxic storm
D. Switch methimazole immediately to oral propylthiouracil

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Immediately discontinue methimazole and obtain an emergent complete blood count with differential

Agranulocytosis (absolute neutrophil count <500/mcL) is a rare but life-threatening idiosyncratic complication of methimazole occurring in 0.3-0.5% of patients. Any child on antithyroid medication who develops acute fever or severe sore throat must stop the drug immediately and have an urgent CBC with differential to rule out neutropenia. If agranulocytosis is confirmed, the drug is permanently discontinued and broad-spectrum antibiotics/G-CSF are initiated.

## Authored MCQ

A 10-year-old girl is referred for evaluation of declining height velocity. Over the past 2 years, her height percentile has dropped from the 50th to the 10th percentile, while her weight percentile has increased from the 45th to the 75th percentile. On physical exam, she has dry skin, sluggish deep tendon reflexes, and a firm, non-tender, lobulated goiter. Bone age is delayed by 3.5 years. What is the most likely underlying diagnosis?

A. Cushing syndrome due to an ACTH-secreting pituitary microadenoma
B. Chronic autoimmune (Hashimoto) thyroiditis
C. Isolated growth hormone neurosecretory dysfunction
D. Graves' disease with autonomous toxic nodule

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Chronic autoimmune (Hashimoto) thyroiditis

The classic growth pattern of juvenile acquired hypothyroidism (most commonly Hashimoto thyroiditis) is marked linear growth deceleration with preserved or increasing weight-for-height (crossing height percentiles downward while BMI increases), accompanied by severe bone age retardation and a firm, lobulated goiter. Cushing syndrome also causes growth failure with obesity, but is not accompanied by a lobulated goiter or marked bone age delay of this degree.

## Authored MCQ

An 8-year-old girl presents with significant bilateral breast enlargement (Tanner stage 3) and an episode of vaginal bleeding. Her height is at the 15th percentile (down from the 50th percentile 2 years ago), and bone age is delayed by 2 years. Pelvic ultrasound reveals enlarged multicystic ovaries. Thyroid panel reveals TSH 480 mIU/L and free T4 0.1 ng/dL. What is the term and physiological mechanism for this clinical condition?

A. McCune-Albright syndrome due to GNAS mutation
B. Van Wyk-Grumbach syndrome due to TSH cross-reactivity at the ovarian FSH receptor
C. Central precocious puberty due to hypothalamic hamartoma
D. Autonomous granulosa cell tumor secreting estradiol

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Van Wyk-Grumbach syndrome due to TSH cross-reactivity at the ovarian FSH receptor

Van Wyk-Grumbach syndrome is a rare manifestation of long-standing, severe, untreated primary juvenile hypothyroidism characterized by isosexual pseudoprecocious puberty (breast development, vaginal bleeding, multicystic ovaries) occurring paradoxically with delayed linear growth and delayed bone age. The extreme elevation of circulating TSH exhibits weak cross-reactivity and agonism at the G-protein-coupled FSH receptor in the ovaries. Prompt treatment with levothyroxine completely reverses the ovarian cysts, pubertal signs, and elevated TSH.

## Authored MCQ

A pregnant woman at 36 weeks of gestation with Graves' disease treated with methimazole delivers a male infant. At day 4 of life, the infant becomes irritable, with a resting heart rate of 190 bpm, poor feeding, diaphoresis, and a small palpable goiter. Serum free T4 is markedly elevated at 4.6 ng/dL with TSH <0.02 mIU/L. What is the primary etiology of this infant's thyrotoxicosis?

A. Autonomous activating mutation in the neonatal TSH receptor gene
B. Transplacental transfer of maternal thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins (TSI / TRAb)
C. Neonatal thyroid adenoma
D. Neonatal iodine-induced hyperthyroidism (Jod-Basedow)

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Transplacental transfer of maternal thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins (TSI / TRAb)

Neonatal Graves' disease is caused by the passive transplacental passage of maternal IgG thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins (TSI/TRAb) during pregnancy. Clinical onset is often delayed until day 2-7 of life as transplacentally transferred maternal antithyroid drug (methimazole) clears from the infant's circulation. It is self-limited, typically resolving in 2 to 4 months as maternal IgG antibodies are degraded.
