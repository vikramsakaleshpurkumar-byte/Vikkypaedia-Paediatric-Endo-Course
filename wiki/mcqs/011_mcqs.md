A 12-day-old male infant born at term presents with poor feeding, lethargy, and a 15% weight loss from birth. He has normal male external genitalia with bilateral descended testes and hyperpigmentation of the scrotum. Serum electrolytes demonstrate sodium 116 mEq/L and potassium 8.2 mEq/L. Blood glucose is 48 mg/dL. What diagnostic finding confirms the most likely diagnosis?

A. Markedly elevated serum 17-hydroxyprogesterone (>10,000 ng/dL) and high plasma renin activity
B. Elevated plasma aldosterone with suppressed plasma renin activity
C. Low ACTH with elevated serum cortisol (>35 mcg/dL)
D. Elevated 11-deoxycorticosterone with metabolic alkalosis

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Markedly elevated serum 17-hydroxyprogesterone (>10,000 ng/dL) and high plasma renin activity

The male infant exhibits acute salt-wasting 21-hydroxylase deficiency presenting in the second week of life with hyperkalemia, severe hyponatremia, and scrotal hyperpigmentation. Massive elevation of 17-OHP and elevated plasma renin confirm classic salt-wasting CAH.

## Authored MCQ

A full-term newborn is noted to have ambiguous genitalia: marked clitoromegaly with a glanular groove, complete labial fusion, and a single perineal urogenital orifice (Prader stage IV). Neither gonad is palpable in the labioscrotal folds. Peripheral blood karyotype reveals 46,XX. What is the underlying hormonal mechanism producing this phenotype?

A. Inability to 21-hydroxylate progesterone precursors, resulting in massive ACTH stimulation and shunting into adrenal androgen biosynthesis in utero
B. Complete peripheral end-organ resistance to dihydrotestosterone due to an androgen receptor mutation
C. Autonomous secretion of testosterone from an undifferentiated dysgenetic intra-abdominal ovary
D. Placental aromatase hyperactivation converting fetal estrogens into testosterone

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Inability to 21-hydroxylate progesterone precursors, resulting in massive ACTH stimulation and shunting into adrenal androgen biosynthesis in utero

In 46,XX classic 21-hydroxylase deficiency, impaired cortisol synthesis removes negative feedback on the pituitary, causing massive ACTH secretion. Accumulated precursors are shunted into androstenedione and testosterone, virilizing the bipotential external genitalia in utero.

## Authored MCQ

A 16-year-old male with classic salt-wasting CAH maintained on hydrocortisone and fludrocortisone undergoes routine evaluation. Testicular examination reveals firm, painless, multinodular lobulated masses bilaterally in the medial aspect of the testes adjacent to the mediastinum testis. Serum 17-OHP is 4,200 ng/dL (reference <200 ng/dL) and androstenedione is elevated. Testicular ultrasound confirms bilateral hypoechoic lobulated lesions. What is the diagnosis and correct clinical management?

A. Testicular Adrenal Rest Tumors (TART); optimize compliance and intensify glucocorticoid suppression to regress the lesions
B. Bilateral malignant testicular seminomas; proceed with immediate bilateral radical orchiectomy
C. Bilateral epididymal cysts; observe with annual scrotal palpation alone
D. Leydig cell adenomas; initiate high-dose mitotane chemotherapy

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Testicular Adrenal Rest Tumors (TART); optimize compliance and intensify glucocorticoid suppression to regress the lesions

Testicular adrenal rest tumors (TART) are benign ACTH-responsive lesions arising from aberrant adrenal cortical cells in the testes of males with CAH. Poor endocrine control (elevated ACTH/17-OHP) stimulates tumor hypertrophy. Increasing glucocorticoid therapy to suppress ACTH leads to tumor regression and preserves fertility.

## Authored MCQ

A 17-year-old female presents with severe facial acne, male-pattern terminal hair growth on the chin and lower abdomen (Ferriman-Gallwey score 12), and oligomenorrhea (3 periods per year). Baseline morning 17-OHP is 380 ng/dL (reference <100 ng/dL). An intravenous cosyntropin stimulation test (250 mcg) produces a 60-minute 17-OHP of 2,400 ng/dL. What is the definitive diagnosis?

A. Non-classic (late-onset) Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia
B. Polycystic Ovary Syndrome with normal adrenal steroidogenesis
C. Cushing disease secondary to pituitary ACTH macroadenoma
D. Primary hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (premature ovarian failure)

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Non-classic (late-onset) Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia

A cosyntropin-stimulated 17-OHP level >1,500 ng/dL (and typically >1,000-2,000 ng/dL) is diagnostic of non-classic 21-hydroxylase deficiency, which commonly masquerades as PCOS in adolescent and young adult females.

## Authored MCQ

A 3-week-old female infant with confirmed classic simple-virilizing CAH (46,XX, Prader III) is prescribed maintenance therapy. Her serum electrolytes and blood pressure are normal. Which medication regimen should be prescribed for ongoing maintenance?

A. Oral hydrocortisone 10–15 mg/m2/day divided in three daily doses, with fludrocortisone monitoring
B. High-dose oral dexamethasone 2 mg daily given as a single nighttime dose
C. Spironolactone monotherapy at 5 mg/kg/day without glucocorticoid replacement
D. Continuous subcutaneous infusion of octreotide at 20 mcg/kg/day

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Oral hydrocortisone 10–15 mg/m2/day divided in three daily doses, with fludrocortisone monitoring

In growing infants and children with classic CAH, short-acting hydrocortisone (10-15 mg/m2/day divided three times daily) is the preferred glucocorticoid because longer-acting agents (dexamethasone, prednisone) cause severe linear growth suppression and metabolic toxicity.
