## Authored MCQ

A 15-year-old girl is evaluated for irregular menses. Menarche occurred at age 12, but over the past 2 years she has had only 3 menstrual periods per year (cycle intervals 60-90 days). On examination, BMI is 28.5 kg/m2 (96th percentile), she has moderate inflammatory facial acne, and terminal hair growth on the upper lip, chin, and lower abdomen (modified Ferriman-Gallwey score 9). Serum total testosterone is elevated at 68 ng/dL (reference: 15-45 ng/dL). Pelvic ultrasound reveals multifollicular ovaries bilaterally. 17-OHP is 110 ng/dL, TSH is 2.1 mIU/L, and prolactin is normal. What is the diagnosis?

A. Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
B. Non-classic congenital adrenal hyperplasia
C. Cushing syndrome
D. Normal physiological adolescent anovulation

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)

This adolescent meets consensus pediatric criteria for Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS): (1) ovulatory dysfunction (oligomenorrhea with cycles >45 days >2 years post-menarche); and (2) both clinical hyperandrogenism (mFG score 9, severe acne) and biochemical hyperandrogenism (elevated testosterone), with exclusion of NCAH (17-OHP <200 ng/dL), thyroid disease, and hyperprolactinemia.

## Authored MCQ

Why do international pediatric endocrinology consensus guidelines specifically exclude polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound from the diagnostic criteria for PCOS in adolescents?

A. Multifollicular ovarian morphology is a normal physiological finding in up to 30-40% of healthy ovulating teenagers
B. Pelvic ultrasound cannot visualize ovaries in adolescent females
C. Ovarian cysts in adolescents always represent malignant teratomas
D. Transvaginal ultrasound is mandatory and cannot be performed in adolescents

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Multifollicular ovarian morphology is a normal physiological finding in up to 30-40% of healthy ovulating teenagers

During normal adolescent pubertal development, developing follicles frequently impart a multifollicular or polycystic appearance to ovaries in 30-40% of completely healthy, regularly cycling teenagers. Using ultrasound morphology as a diagnostic criterion in adolescents creates high rates of false-positive overdiagnosis. Thus, guidelines require both ovulatory dysfunction and hyperandrogenism, omitting ultrasound morphology.

## Authored MCQ

What is the primary mechanism by which combined oral contraceptive pills (COCs) improve hirsutism and hyperandrogenemia in adolescents with PCOS?

A. Suppression of pituitary LH secretion and stimulation of hepatic sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) synthesis
B. Direct irreversible inhibition of ovarian 17-alpha-hydroxylase
C. Selective destruction of the adrenal zona reticularis
D. Degradation of circulating dihydrotestosterone by renal tubular filtration

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Suppression of pituitary LH secretion and stimulation of hepatic sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) synthesis

Combined oral contraceptives improve hyperandrogenism through a dual mechanism: (1) the progestin component suppresses pituitary LH pulse frequency, decreasing LH-stimulated ovarian theca cell androgen production; and (2) the estrogen component stimulates hepatic synthesis of SHBG, which binds circulating testosterone and sharply lowers the biologically active free testosterone fraction.

## Authored MCQ

A 16-year-old girl presents with severe, rapidly progressive hirsutism, temporal hair thinning, and deepening of her voice over the past 4 months. Physical examination reveals a 2.5-cm clitoromegaly. Serum total testosterone is markedly elevated at 280 ng/dL (reference: 15-45 ng/dL). DHEAS is normal. Pelvic ultrasound reveals a 4.5-cm solid vascular mass within the right ovary. What is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Severe polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
B. Sertoli-Leydig cell tumor of the ovary
C. Non-classic congenital adrenal hyperplasia
D. Adrenal adenoma secreting DHEAS

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Sertoli-Leydig cell tumor of the ovary

Rapid onset of virilization (clitoromegaly, deepening voice, male-pattern alopecia), massive elevation of serum total testosterone (>150-200 ng/dL), and a solid unilateral ovarian mass is pathognomonic for an ovarian androgen-secreting neoplasm, most commonly a Sertoli-Leydig cell tumor. PCOS does not cause rapid virilization, clitoromegaly, or a solid unilateral ovarian mass.

## Authored MCQ

A 16-year-old female high school distance runner presents with secondary amenorrhea for 8 months. She runs 50 miles per week and has restricted her caloric intake. BMI is 17.2 kg/m2. Examination shows Tanner 5 breasts and pubic hair without hirsutism or acne. Pregnancy test is negative. Serum LH is 0.8 IU/L, FSH is 2.4 IU/L, estradiol is 18 pg/mL (low), and total testosterone is 12 ng/dL (low-normal). What is the underlying pathophysiology?

A. Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea (FHA) due to relative energy deficiency in sport (RED-S)
B. Polycystic ovary syndrome with atypical presentation
C. Premature ovarian insufficiency (POF)
D. Androgen insensitivity syndrome

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea (FHA) due to relative energy deficiency in sport (RED-S)

Functional hypothalamic amenorrhea (FHA) in athletes is caused by chronic low energy availability (energy expenditure exceeding dietary intake, known as Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport [RED-S]). This suppresses hypothalamic GnRH pulsatility, leading to low/suppressed LH and FSH, hypoestrogenemia (estradiol <20-30 pg/mL), and secondary amenorrhea. In contrast to PCOS, patients with FHA are typically underweight/lean and have low (not high) androgens without hirsutism.
