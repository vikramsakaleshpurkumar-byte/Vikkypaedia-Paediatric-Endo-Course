## Authored MCQ

An 8-year-old girl presents to the emergency department with a 3-day history of polydipsia, polyuria, abdominal pain, and Kussmaul respirations. Point-of-care glucose is 480 mg/dL. Venous blood gas reveals pH 7.12, pCO2 18 mmHg, bicarbonate 8 mmol/L, sodium 132 mEq/L, potassium 4.8 mEq/L, and blood beta-hydroxybutyrate 5.8 mmol/L. She is alert but tachypneic. After administering an initial 10 mL/kg bolus of 0.9% normal saline over 1 hour, what is the most appropriate next step in her medical management?

A. Administer an IV bolus of regular insulin at 0.1 units/kg followed by continuous infusion
B. Initiate continuous regular insulin infusion at 0.05-0.1 units/kg/hour without an insulin bolus, and plan fluid replacement over 24-48 hours
C. Administer intravenous sodium bicarbonate 1 mEq/kg to correct severe acidosis
D. Add 10% dextrose to IV fluids immediately

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Initiate continuous regular insulin infusion at 0.05-0.1 units/kg/hour without an insulin bolus, and plan fluid replacement over 24-48 hours

International consensus guidelines (ISPAD/PEDS) dictate that in pediatric moderate-to-severe DKA: (1) Insulin should be administered as a continuous regular insulin infusion at 0.05-0.1 units/kg/hour started 1-2 hours after beginning IV fluid resuscitation; (2) An IV insulin bolus is strictly contraindicated because it causes sudden osmotic shifts and triggers hypokalemia, increasing cerebral edema risk; (3) Fluid replacement must be distributed evenly over 24-48 hours; (4) Bicarbonate is contraindicated due to paradoxical CSF acidosis and hypokalemia.

## Authored MCQ

During management of severe DKA in a 10-year-old boy, blood glucose drops from 480 mg/dL to 260 mg/dL after 4 hours of IV fluids and continuous regular insulin at 0.1 units/kg/hour. Venous blood gas shows persistent acidosis: pH 7.18, bicarbonate 11 mmol/L, and urine ketones remain large. What is the most appropriate next step?

A. Stop the insulin infusion because the blood glucose is approaching normal
B. Reduce insulin infusion to 0.01 units/kg/hour and administer subcutaneous rapid-acting insulin
C. Add 5% dextrose to the IV hydration fluids and continue the insulin infusion at 0.05-0.1 units/kg/hour to clear the ketoacidosis
D. Administer IV sodium bicarbonate to accelerate acidosis resolution

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Add 5% dextrose to the IV hydration fluids and continue the insulin infusion at 0.05-0.1 units/kg/hour to clear the ketoacidosis

In DKA, insulin is required not merely to lower blood glucose, but fundamentally to suppress lipolysis, suppress ketogenesis, and stimulate peripheral ketone clearance. When blood glucose approaches 250-300 mg/dL, 5% dextrose (and later 10% dextrose if needed) must be added to the IV fluids while maintaining the insulin infusion at 0.05-0.1 U/kg/hr. This prevents hypoglycemia while allowing insulin to completely resolve the ketoacidosis.

## Authored MCQ

Six hours into treatment for severe DKA, an 11-year-old girl complains of a severe, worsening headache. On exam, her heart rate has dropped from 110 bpm to 58 bpm, blood pressure has risen from 104/65 to 138/88 mmHg, and she is difficult to arouse. What is the most critical immediate intervention?

A. Transport the patient immediately to the radiology suite for an emergent unenhanced head CT
B. Administer intravenous 3% hypertonic saline (3-5 mL/kg over 10-15 minutes) or 20% mannitol immediately at the bedside
C. Increase the rate of 0.9% normal saline infusion to treat hypovolemia
D. Administer intravenous lorazepam for suspected subclinical status epilepticus

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Administer intravenous 3% hypertonic saline (3-5 mL/kg over 10-15 minutes) or 20% mannitol immediately at the bedside

The sudden onset of severe headache, bradycardia, and hypertension (Cushing triad) with neurological deterioration represents impending cerebral herniation from DKA-related cerebral edema. Cerebral edema is a medical emergency with high mortality. Treatment with hyperosmolar therapy (3% hypertonic saline 3-5 mL/kg or 20% mannitol 0.5-1.0 g/kg) must be administered immediately at the bedside; diagnostic imaging must NEVER delay treatment.

## Authored MCQ

A 13-year-old male with established type 1 diabetes weighs 50 kg and takes a total daily dose of 40 units of insulin. Using the 500 Rule and 1800 Rule, calculate his estimated Insulin-to-Carbohydrate Ratio (ICR) and Insulin Sensitivity Factor (ISF):

A. ICR: 1 unit per 12.5 g carbohydrate; ISF: 1 unit lowers blood glucose by 45 mg/dL
B. ICR: 1 unit per 5 g carbohydrate; ISF: 1 unit lowers blood glucose by 20 mg/dL
C. ICR: 1 unit per 25 g carbohydrate; ISF: 1 unit lowers blood glucose by 70 mg/dL
D. ICR: 1 unit per 15 g carbohydrate; ISF: 1 unit lowers blood glucose by 90 mg/dL

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

ICR: 1 unit per 12.5 g carbohydrate; ISF: 1 unit lowers blood glucose by 45 mg/dL

The standard clinical formulas for estimating starting pump and MDI parameters based on Total Daily Dose (TDD = 40 units) are: (1) 500 Rule for ICR = 500 / TDD = 500 / 40 = 12.5 grams of carbohydrate per 1 unit of rapid-acting insulin; (2) 1800 Rule for ISF = 1800 / TDD = 1800 / 40 = 45 mg/dL reduction in blood glucose per 1 unit of rapid-acting insulin.

## Authored MCQ

A 10-year-old boy with type 1 diabetes diagnosed 6 months ago has an HbA1c of 6.2% on a total daily insulin dose of 0.25 units/kg/day. His parents ask if he has been cured of diabetes. What is the explanation for his current low insulin requirement?

A. He is experiencing the 'honeymoon' (partial remission) phase due to temporary recovery of surviving beta cells
B. He was misdiagnosed and actually has maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY)
C. He has developed complete insulin sensitivity due to low carbohydrate intake
D. His islet autoantibodies have permanently disappeared

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

He is experiencing the 'honeymoon' (partial remission) phase due to temporary recovery of surviving beta cells

The 'honeymoon' or partial clinical remission phase occurs in up to 60-80% of children shortly after diagnosis and initial glycemic stabilization. Relief of glucotoxicity allows surviving beta cells to temporarily produce sufficient endogenous insulin to maintain near-normal glycemic control with very low exogenous insulin requirements (<0.5 units/kg/day). This phase typically lasts several months to a year before autoimmune destruction inevitably progresses to absolute insulin deficiency.
