## Authored MCQ

A 9-year-old boy is evaluated for learning difficulties and hyperactivity. Routine screening reveals free T4 2.8 ng/dL (reference: 0.9-1.8 ng/dL), free T3 6.4 pg/mL (reference: 2.3-4.2 pg/mL), and TSH 3.8 mIU/L (reference: 0.5-4.5 mIU/L). Examination reveals a resting heart rate of 105 bpm and a mild, diffuse, non-tender goiter without orbitopathy. His mother and maternal uncle are reported to have similar laboratory findings and were erroneously treated with radioactive iodine in the past. What is the most likely molecular defect?

A. Heterozygous loss-of-function mutation in the TSHR gene
B. Heterozygous dominant-negative mutation in the THRB gene
C. Hemizygous inactivating mutation in the SLC16A2 (MCT8) gene
D. Activating mutation in the GNAS gene

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Heterozygous dominant-negative mutation in the THRB gene

Resistance to thyroid hormone beta (RTH-beta) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by mutations in the THRB gene. It is characterized by elevated free T4 and free T3 with an unsuppressed (normal or slightly elevated) TSH. Due to differential receptor expression, TR-alpha in the heart is stimulated by elevated hormones, causing resting sinus tachycardia, while goiter arises from unsuppressed TSH. Autosomal dominant transmission in the mother and uncle confirms RTH-beta.

## Authored MCQ

A 16-year-old girl is found to have elevated free T4 (2.6 ng/dL) and free T3 (5.8 pg/mL) with a serum TSH of 4.5 mIU/L. To differentiate between resistance to thyroid hormone beta (RTH-beta) and a TSH-secreting pituitary neuroendocrine tumor (TSHoma), which diagnostic finding would most strongly support a TSHoma?

A. A markedly elevated glycoprotein hormone alpha-subunit concentration and alpha-subunit/TSH ratio >1.0
B. A 3-fold rise in serum TSH in response to an intravenous TRH stimulation test
C. Presence of a heterozygous mutation in the THRB gene
D. Complete absence of symptoms of thyrotoxicosis

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

A markedly elevated glycoprotein hormone alpha-subunit concentration and alpha-subunit/TSH ratio >1.0

Both RTH-beta and TSHomas present with high free T4/T3 and unsuppressed TSH. A TSHoma is distinguished by an elevated circulating pituitary glycoprotein hormone alpha-subunit level and an alpha-subunit/TSH molar ratio >1.0 (present in >80% of TSHomas), blunted TSH response to TRH stimulation, and a visible pituitary lesion on contrast MRI. In contrast, RTH-beta typically shows a normal alpha-subunit ratio and a brisk, normal TSH rise to TRH.

## Authored MCQ

A 7-month-old infant boy is evaluated for profound hypotonia, failure to achieve head control, and severe developmental delay. Examination reveals generalized weakness, spasticity in lower extremities, dystonic posturing, and poor weight gain. Laboratory testing demonstrates serum total T3 380 ng/dL (reference: 105-245 ng/dL), free T4 0.5 ng/dL (reference: 0.9-1.8 ng/dL), and TSH 3.2 mIU/L. What is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Resistance to thyroid hormone beta (RTH-beta)
B. Allan-Herndon-Dudley syndrome (MCT8 deficiency)
C. Congenital hypopituitarism with isolated TSH deficiency
D. Thyroid dysgenesis with lingual thyroid

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Allan-Herndon-Dudley syndrome (MCT8 deficiency)

Allan-Herndon-Dudley syndrome is an X-linked inherited neurodevelopmental disorder caused by loss-of-function mutations in SLC16A2 (encoding MCT8), which mediates thyroid hormone transport into neurons. Affected males exhibit severe cognitive disability, central hypotonia, progressive spasticity, dystonia, and a pathognomonic serum thyroid panel: high total and free T3, low free T4, and normal or slightly elevated TSH.

## Authored MCQ

A 5-year-old child presents with severe skeletal dysplasia, delayed fontanel closure, delayed tooth eruption, marked constipation, and bradycardia. Biochemical evaluation reveals normal TSH (2.1 mIU/L), low-normal free T4 (0.8 ng/dL), high-normal free T3 (4.4 pg/mL), a markedly reduced free T4 to free T3 ratio, and very low reverse T3. What receptor is defective in this child?

A. Thyroid hormone receptor beta (TR-beta)
B. Thyroid hormone receptor alpha (TR-alpha1)
C. TSH receptor (TSHR)
D. Glucocorticoid receptor (GR)

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Thyroid hormone receptor alpha (TR-alpha1)

Resistance to thyroid hormone alpha (RTH-alpha) is caused by heterozygous mutations in THRA. Because TR-alpha1 is heavily expressed in bone, brain, and intestine, while TR-beta regulates pituitary feedback, patients exhibit tissue-specific hypothyroid symptoms (skeletal dysplasia, delayed tooth eruption, macrocephaly, constipation, bradycardia) with a distinctive hormonal signature: near-normal TSH, low-normal FT4, high FT3, low FT4/FT3 ratio, and extremely low rT3.

## Authored MCQ

An 11-year-old asymptomatic boy is diagnosed with RTH-beta after genetic testing confirms a heterozygous pathogenic mutation in THRB. His free T4 is 2.5 ng/dL, free T3 is 5.2 pg/mL, and TSH is 3.1 mIU/L. His resting pulse is 80 bpm and height is tracking along the 50th percentile. Which management strategy is most appropriate?

A. Immediate radioactive iodine ablation to normalize circulating thyroid hormones
B. Observation and clinical monitoring without antithyroid medications or levothyroxine
C. High-dose methimazole to normalize free T4 and free T3
D. Subtotal thyroidectomy to treat subclinical thyrotoxicosis

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Observation and clinical monitoring without antithyroid medications or levothyroxine

In asymptomatic patients with RTH-beta who have normal growth and no cardiovascular compromise, no pharmacological treatment is required. Elevated circulating thyroid hormone levels are necessary to overcome the end-organ receptor resistance. Attempting to lower thyroid hormone levels with antithyroid drugs, radioactive iodine, or surgery causes pituitary TSH overdrive, massive goiter expansion, and severe tissue hypothyroidism.
