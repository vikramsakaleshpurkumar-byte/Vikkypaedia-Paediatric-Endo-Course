## Authored MCQ

A 9-month-old infant with genetically confirmed Prader-Willi syndrome (paternal 15q11-q13 deletion) is evaluated for somatropin therapy. He has marked hypotonia, poor head control, and short stature (length SDS -2.3). Before prescribing somatropin, which diagnostic investigation is mandatory to establish treatment safety?

A. Overnight polysomnography and ENT evaluation for adenotonsillar hypertrophy
B. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry to assess total body calcium
C. 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring
D. Lumbar puncture to rule out elevated intracranial pressure

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Overnight polysomnography and ENT evaluation for adenotonsillar hypertrophy

Because somatropin can stimulate lymphoid tissue proliferation and potentially exacerbate preexisting upper airway obstruction in hypotonic infants with PWS, baseline polysomnography and ENT assessment are mandatory before initiation.

## Authored MCQ

A 4-year-old boy with Prader-Willi syndrome has been treated with somatropin (1.0 mg/m2/day) for 2 years. His parents ask what measurable changes in body composition have been established by long-term clinical trials. What is the most accurate response?

A. Statistically significant increase in lean muscle mass and sustained reduction in body fat percentage
B. Total cure of hyperphagia with normalization of satiety signaling
C. Complete conversion of white adipose tissue to functional brown adipose tissue
D. No measurable change in body composition despite acceleration of linear height

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Statistically significant increase in lean muscle mass and sustained reduction in body fat percentage

Extensive randomized and longitudinal trials demonstrate that somatropin therapy significantly improves body composition in PWS by increasing lean muscle mass, decreasing fat mass, and boosting resting energy expenditure, though it does not abolish underlying hypothalamic hyperphagia.

## Authored MCQ

A 2-year-old girl with Prader-Willi syndrome is undergoing consideration for somatropin therapy. Pre-treatment polysomnography reveals severe obstructive sleep apnea with an apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) of 22 events/hour and oxygen desaturations to 74%. Examination reveals 4+ kissing tonsils. What is the correct clinical management?

A. Defer somatropin, refer for adenotonsillectomy, and re-evaluate with repeat polysomnography postoperatively
B. Start somatropin immediately at double dose to strengthen pharyngeal musculature
C. Initiate continuous mecasermin therapy as an alternative to somatropin
D. Administer high-dose dexamethasone for 2 weeks followed by immediate somatropin

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Defer somatropin, refer for adenotonsillectomy, and re-evaluate with repeat polysomnography postoperatively

Severe obstructive sleep apnea is a contraindication to starting somatropin. Airway obstruction must be resolved surgically (adenotonsillectomy) or with positive airway pressure, with post-intervention polysomnography confirming resolution before somatropin can be safely initiated.

## Authored MCQ

A 14-month-old boy with Prader-Willi syndrome initiates somatropin at 0.5 mg/m2/day. Six weeks into therapy, his mother reports that he has developed new-onset loud snoring and mouth breathing during sleep. What is the most appropriate next step in management?

A. Suspend somatropin therapy and perform urgent ENT examination and polysomnography
B. Reassure the mother that snoring indicates expanding respiratory vital capacity
C. Increase the somatropin dose to 1.5 mg/m2/day to accelerate laryngeal maturation
D. Prescribe over-the-counter diphenhydramine at bedtime

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Suspend somatropin therapy and perform urgent ENT examination and polysomnography

New-onset or worsening sleep-disordered breathing in a child with PWS following somatropin initiation warrants temporary suspension of therapy, clinical ENT evaluation for adenoid/tonsillar enlargement, and repeat polysomnography.

## Authored MCQ

Which genetic mechanism underlying Prader-Willi syndrome carries a higher documented risk of maternal age-related meiotic non-disjunction and a lower risk of hypopigmentation compared to paternal deletions?

A. Maternal uniparental disomy 15 (UPD15)
B. Paternal 15q11.2-q13 microdeletion
C. Imprinting center microdeletion
D. Balanced paternal translocation involving 15q

<details><summary>Answer and rationale</summary>

Maternal uniparental disomy 15 (UPD15)

Maternal uniparental disomy (UPD15) accounts for ~25% of PWS cases and is associated with advanced maternal age. Because both OCA2 genes are retained, these children do not exhibit the marked hypopigmentation seen in large paternal 15q deletions.
