### 36.2 Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia

### Type 1 (MEN1)

### 36.2.1 Introduction

MEN1 is a rare endocrine tumour predisposi tion syndrome with an approximate preva lence of 1 in 30,000 individuals. The clinical signs of MEN1—including the main manifes tations of pituitary, parathyroid and neuroen docrine tumours—were first described as early as 1903 by Erdheim and was defined by Underdahl and Werner five decades later [1–3]. Wermer proposed that the cause of the disease—first named Wermer-syndrome—is due to a single genetic defect with autosomal inheritance; later, it was renamed as multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 [3].

MEN1 syndrome is autosomal domi nantly inherited with an overall close to 100% penetrance. In the vast majority of the cases, heterozygous loss-of-function mutations can be identified in the MEN1 gene. Development of different tumours in MEN1 follow the “two-hit” theory suggested by Knudson for retinoblastoma [4]. Affected individuals inherit one mutated copy of the MEN1 gene from one parent (first hit) and one normal copy from the other parent. During tumori genesis in susceptible tissues, there is a somatic mutational event, representing the second hit, affecting the inherited normal copy of the gene MEN1 gene (such as a loss-of-function point mutation, loss of part of chromosome 11q13, or a change affecting the promoter of the gene or a change in regulatory microR NAs miR-24-1) and tumours develop in those somatic cells which are left with no normal copy/normal expression of the gene [5]. In some 10% of MEN1 syndrome patients the germline mutations develop de novo.

Data from five retrospective paediatric cohorts have been reviewed recently regarding MEN1 [6–11]. The two largest cohorts are the French database (Groupe d’étude des Tumeurs Endocrine, GTE) and an international cohort study with participating centres in the United Kingdom, Austria and USA [7, 11]. In the GTE study, clinical data of 160 MEN1

patients below the age of 21 years were pre sented having at least one manifestation of the syndrome. Age at manifestation, descrip tion of the first symptoms (clinical symptoms, biological or imaging abnormalities) and data on surgical outcomes, were analysed [7]. The international study describes data of 80 MEN1 carriers before the age of <18 years. Fifty-six patients (70%) developed a MEN1related endocrine tumour, and they gave information on the clinical characteristics of these tumours [11]. Here, we summarised pae diatric MEN1 data mainly from these two cohorts.

Diagnosis of MEN1 is a clinical diagnosis having two out of the three main MEN1 man ifestations. Pathogenic alteration in the MEN1 gene is not necessary for the diagnosis. For familial diagnosis, patient should have one MEN1-related tumour plus first degree rela tive with MEN1. For genetic diagnosis, a germline mutation of MEN1 has to be detected, but more recently, other genes have also been identified with an MEN1-like phe notype, including MEN4 and MEN5.

The three main manifestations of MEN1 are parathyroid adenomas, duodenopancre atic neuroendocrine tumours and anterior pituitary adenomas (pituitary neuroendocrine tumours, PitNETs). The most important manifestations of MEN1 in childhood are primary hyperparathyroidism (pHPT), pro lactinomas, non-functioning pituitary adeno mas (NFPAs), functioning (insulinoma, GHRH) or non-functioning pancreas neuro endocrine tumours (NF-PANNETs), and rarely thymic tumours. MEN1-related endo crine tumours have a different clinical course from sporadic tumours in manifesting earlier and having a multifocal presentation of the same organ [6, 12, 13].

The first symptoms in the GTE cohort were related to pHPT in 75%, pituitary ade nomas in 34%, non-secreting pancreatic tumours in 9%, insulinomas in 12%, gastrino mas in 2%, malignant adrenal tumours in 1%, and a malignant thymic-NET in one case (1%). Hyperparathyroidism was not always the first lesion; it developed first in 56% of the patients. The first symptoms occurred before the age of 10 years in 14% and before 5 years

in 3% [7]. In the international cohort, 30% of patients with MEN1 did not present clinical signs of the disease by the age of 18 years. Fifty-six patients (70%) developed an endo crine tumour by the age of 18 years (median age 14 years, range 6–18 years). Primary hyperparathyroidism occurred in >80% of patients, Pancreaticoduodenal neuroendo crine tumours developed in ~35% of patients, of whom 70% had non-functioning tumours, 35% had insulinomas, and 5% had gastrino mas, with ~15% having metastases and 55% undergoing surgery. Pituitary tumours devel oped in 30% of patients, and ~35% of these tumours were macroprolactinomas. Tumour occurrence in male and female patients was not significantly different [11]. There are no clear genotype-phenotype correlations, and within the same family, tumour type and age of manifestation are highly different. Malignant presentations of tumours are rare in the paediatric age, but presented in a few cases.

Thymic NETs are one of the most aggres sive MEN1-related tumours. Thymus NETs are rare in childhood (1 of the 160 cases in GTE study with manifestation at the age of 16 years, this is the earliest case in the litera ture) and none presented in the series of the international study group [7].

Adrenal tumours are also rare in children, two patients were reported in the GTE cohort (age 4 and 16 years, both adrenocortical carci nomas) and one in the international cohort. The earliest patient reported suffering from adrenocortical tumour was 3 years old [14]. There are only few MEN1-related cases of bronchial tumours in childhood the youngest patient being 15 years. Patients with MEN1 have a decreased life-expectancy mainly due to development of malignant duodenopan creatic and thymus.

It has been reported that MEN1 mutation negative patients have less aggressive disease, often with a smaller number of organs affected. The median age of first and second tumours development has been reported approximately 10 years later compared to MEN1 mutation–positive patients [15, 16].

### 36.2.2 Primary

### Hyperparathyroidism

Primary hyperthyroidism is the most common (56% of cases) and often the earliest manifes tation of MEN1 in children. In most cases, it is diagnosed as a result of hypercalcaemia on routine or surveillance-driven biochemical screening in asymptomatic patients. The earli est age of manifestation in the literature is 4 years. In contrast to isolated non-MEN1related hyperparathyroidism, it is usually caused by multifocal gland hyperplasia or multiple adenomas of the four main parathy roid glands, and also of some ‘supernumer ary’ and ectopic glands. No malignant parathyroid tumour has been described in children. The male-female ratio is equal. In the GTE study, the mean age at manifestation of hyperparathyroidism was 16 years: 90% of the pHPT cases occurred after 10 years of age. In patients younger than 21 years of age, 17% of the symptomatic cases presented with uro lithiasis, fatigue and bone pain. In ~80% of cases, pHPT was discovered by biochemical screening [7]. The mean age of manifestation was 15 years (ranged 6–18 years) and 20% of the cases were symptomatic [11].

### 36.2.2.1 Diagnosis

Screening for hyperparathyroidism always needs parallel measurements of serum cal cium and parathormone while patient has normal vitamin D levels. Chronic renal failure and tertiary hyperparathyroidism have to be excluded. Elevated serum calcium levels can be seen with normal or elevated parathor mone levels, and in mild forms of pHPT, nor mal serum total calcium levels can be detected with elevated serum parathormone levels; 24-h urinary calcium collection could be chal lenge in smaller children, this spot urine cal cium concentration is often used.

### 36.2.2.2 Therapy

Definitive treatment is surgical removal of the parathyroid glands, but there is debate on the optimal time and type of surgical approach. Subtotal parathyroidectomy (one half gland

left in situ) or total parathyroidectomy (4 glands and supernumerary and ectopic gland resection) with implantation of parathyroid autograft to the non-dominant forearm) are the different surgical approaches, with trans cervical (partial) thymectomy in an attempt to remove ectopic parathyroid glands from the chest and reduce chances of malignant thymus tumour in MEN1 patients. In cases of subto tal parathyroidectomy, the recurrence rate of hyperparathyroidism is high, but it is also seen with total parathyroidectomy. There needs to be a careful balance of reducing tissue burden, and weighing up the risks to surrounding organs. The use of calcimimetic agents (e.g., cinacalcet) could a form of treatment to diminish hypercalcaemia. Data from 18 paedi atric patients with pHPT have shown that the use of cinacalcet at a dose of 0.6–1.4 mg/kg/ day has a good effect on controlling hypercal caemia and elevated parathormone levels without significant side effects [17].

### 36.2.3 Tumours of the Anterior

### Pituitary

MEN1-related pituitary manifestations include pituitary adenomas (pituitary neuro endocrine tumours, PitNETs), which could be the first manifestation (21%) of disease. These tumours are the second in frequency in chil dren, but rarely develop before the age of 10 years. The known earliest age of manifesta tion is 5 years [18]. Pituitary adenomas are usually prolactinomas and non-functioning microadenomas, but all subtypes of pituitary adenomas have been reported in the literature. Most of the pituitary adenomas are microad enomas, but macroprolactinomas are seen most often in boys. Growth-hormone secret ing tumours are rare in children, and they are usually macroadenomas, but we need to be aware of acromegaly due to GHRH-secreting pancreatic NETs [19]. In the GTE study, the mean age of presentation was 17 years, rang ing from 10 years to 21 years. Half of the patients were symptomatic (55%) [7]. In the international cohort, the median age of pre

sentation was 14 years, ranging from 10 to 18 years [11]. According to tumour types, the two studies showed 62% (GTE) or 80% (inter national study) of the pituitary adenoma cases being prolactinomas, and 25% and 20% non-functioning-pituitary adenomas, with single corticotropinoma and somatotropi noma in the GTE study [7]. The clinical mani festations depend on the type of hormone secretion, such as growth and pubertal abnor malities, galactorrhoea, Cushingoid signs, headache and vision problems. MEN1-related pituitary adenomas are often multihormonal and also double adenomas have been described.

### 36.2.3.1 Diagnosis

In asymptomatic MEN1 children, annual bio chemical screening should start at the age of 5 years and pituitary imaging at the age of 15 years followed by every 3 years [20]. Screening protocols recommend measuring serum prolactin and serum IGF-1 levels to detect prolactin and growth hormonesecreting tumours. In case of an elevated pro lactin level, macroprolactinemia (the presence of so-called biological inactive big-big prolac tin) has to be excluded. Serial dilution can detect the ‘hook effect’ (extreme hyperprolac tinaemia causing false low results through interference of antibody-antigen complex for mation), although this is now rarely a prob lem. In the presence of MRI-identifiable tumours with elevated serum IGF-1 levels (use age- and sex-matched reference ranges), a GH suppression test with glucose load has to be performed. In the case of failure to supress serum GH levels below 1 μg/L during the diagnostic test, a diagnosis of GH is be estab lished. The cut-off level in adults is 0.4 μg/L, but especially in otherwise healthy teenagers, sometimes, higher levels have been described. Pituitary adenomas are demonstrated on ded icated contrast-enhanced pituitary MRI. Routine biochemical screening for hypercortisolism when clinical suspicion is not present is not recommended. About 9–22% of paediatric pituitary adenomas are associated with genetic tumourpredisposing syndromes such as MEN1. Screening for both MEN1 and AIP germline mutations in all paediatric patients with pro lactin and GH-secreting adenomas has been suggested. Algorithms for genetic screening in pituitary adenomas presenting in childhood depending on the family history, pituitary tumour type and age at onset are already available [21, 22].

### 36.2.3.2 Therapy

Therapeutic approaches in MEN1-related pituitary tumours do not differ from treat ment of sporadic forms. Prolactinomas usu ally show a good response to medical therapy with dopamine agonists as first-line therapy and prognosis is good in the majority of the cases. In some cases, after an initial good response, escape has been observed [23, 24]. Cabergoline is the choice of drug in children as well as adults due to its higher efficacy and less side effects. Dose of cabergoline varies between 0.25 and 2 mg per week, but higher doses have been described in severe cases. For all macroprolactinomas before the age of 20 years, dopamine agonist resistance was reported up to 16% of patients, but at this stage, it is unclear if an MEN1 mutation would be an independent predictor of dopa mine agonist resistance [25]. Side effects of dopamine agonists may include cardiac valvu lar fibrosis, especially at prolonged high doses and mood or impulse control problems have also been seen in children. The mechanism for drug-resistance is not clear, it may relate to dopamine receptor down-regulation. In case of intracranial pressure or neuroophthalmological emergencies with worsen ing visual field defect caused by optic chiasma compression, a debulking procedure is needed. In therapy resistant cases, radiotherapy, pos sibly proton beam therapy, can be used. Longterm tumour and treatment-related

### 36.2.4 Duodenopancreatic

### Neuroendocrine Tumours (dpNETs)

DpNETs are the primary life-threatening manifestations of MEN1. Clinical screening (imaging and biochemistry) reveals evidence of enteropancreatic tumours in up to 80% of patients with MEN1 throughout life [13]. These tumours are either associated with clin ical signs of excess hormonal production (gas trin, insulin, glucagon, somatostatin, GHRH, calcitonin and vasoactive intestinal peptide) or are clinically non-functioning (may still stain for some of these hormones) [7, 26]. Manifestations of all duodenopancreatic NETs were 23% in the GTE and 38% in the international study. According to these two databases, non-functioning pancreatic NETs were detected in 9% (mean age 16 years) and 27% (mean age 15) with the youngest patient 10 years old [7, 11]. According to the interna tional cohort data among the dpNETs before the age of 18 years, more than 70% were nonfunctioning tumours, >35% were insulinomas, and <5% were gastrinomas, with 15% having metastases [11].

### 36.2.4.1 Clinical Manifestations

MEN1-related pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours often synthesise multiple hormones, but hormone synthesis does not always have clinical consequences.

Insulinomas manifest at a younger age than gastrinomas and symptoms of hyperin sulinemia can be the first clinical sign of MEN1 in childhood (10% of paediatric cases), although they typically manifest in the second to fourth decade. MEN1-related insulinomas present earlier than sporadic insulinomas. The mean age of presentation is 15 years, the youngest patient being 5 [7]. Insulinomas are symptomatic in all cases and need interven tion [11]. Patients with insulinomas present signs and symptoms of hypoglycaemia, weak

ness, neuroglycopenia (loss of consciousness and seizures) and autonomic symptoms. It is crucial for children and adolescents and their families to be educated as to the signs of hypoglycaemia and neuroglycopaenic syn dromes.

Gastrinomas are rare in childhood (2%), the youngest being 6 years old. Gastrinomas cause Zollinger-Ellison syndrome presenting with abdominal pain, diarrhoea and steator rhoea associated with elevated gastric acid production and recurrent peptic ulcers.

Glucagonomas have been described only in a single childhood case, where it was a silent glucagonoma [9].

Non-functioning pancreatic tumours usu ally require one of the several imaging tech niques. Blood-based tumour markers as chromogranin A and B, gastric inhibitory polypeptide-GIP, pancreatic polypeptide-PP, somatostatin, vasoactive intestinal peptide- VIP, and GHRH provide important informa tion regarding the presence of tumour, but the only available in specialist laboratories. They may be malignant and metastasise, usually to the liver [11]. Non-functioning pancreatic NETs have been detected as early as 12–14 years in asymptomatic children with MEN1 [27].

### 36.2.4.2 Other Manifestations

### in Children

Other manifestations include bronchopulmo nary and thymic neuroendocrine tumours and adrenal adenomas or carcinomas. Nonendocrine MEN1-associated tumours are lesions of the skin (e.g., lipomas, angiofibro mas, collagenomas and hibernomas), of the meninges (meningiomas) and of the epen dyma (ependymomas), leiomyomas and Barrett’s oesophagus. In females, breast can cers were shown in the Dutch MEN1 cohort to have a 2.8 times increased risk compared to the general population, but regular screening is needed in adulthood only from the age of 40 years. Thymic NETs occur in 2.0–8.2% and bronchopulmonary NETs in 5–13% of MEN1 patients, with a median age at diagnosis of 43 and 45 years, respectively. The youngest patient with a thymic NET was 16 years old,

the youngest with a bronchopulmonary NET was 20 years old. These tumours are mostly asymptomatic and discovered incidentally or via screening. Various cutaneous lesions such as multiple facial angiofibromas, collageno mas and lipomas are also common and may be useful in identifying the disease. In general, skin lesions need therapy only in the case of local symptoms. Adrenal tumours, uni- or bilateral, are present in approximately 20% of patients with a median age at diagnosis of 46 years. They may be. The majority of MEN1-associated adrenal tumours are hor monally inactive, but ~15% secrete cortisol, androgens or aldosterone. The youngest MEN1 child with adrenal cancer was 3 years old [11, 12, 20]. In two large series of MEN1 patients, hyperandrogenism was the clinical feature of all adrenal tumours before the age of 20 years and was classified as an adrenal carcinoma [7, 20, 28].

### 36.2.4.3 Diagnosis

For the diagnosis of insulinoma, the gold standard diagnostic test is a 72-h fast with careful monitoring over the whole period. The diagnosis is confirmed if the serum glucose is <3 mmol/L with a parallel finding of serum insulin level of 3 mU/L or higher by immuno chemiluminescent assay and C-peptide of 0.2 nmol/L or higher [20]. If the tumour can not be localised by conventional imaging, arterial calcium stimulation with venous sam pling may be helpful [29]. Benign insulinomas express glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor at high density. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor-based imaging Ga-Exendin-4 PET-CT is very promising in identifying insu linomas [6, 30].

Serum biomarkers, such as chromogranin A, pancreatic polypeptide, somatostatin or glucagon measurements are of low diagnostic value and imaging screening forms the basis for the diagnosis of non-functioning pancreas NETs. The diagnosis of a gastrinoma can be established by an elevated fasting serum gas trin level (often more than 1000 ng/L) with a gastric pH below 2. The use of proton-pump inhibitors should be stopped for at least 4 weeks before measuring gastrin secretion, and

In the case of a borderline gastrin level elevation, a gastrin level of more than 120 ng/L above baseline after administration of secretin (2 μg/ kg intravenously) confirm the diagnosis [20]. Gastrin levels may also be elevated in hyper calcaemia caused by hyperparathyroidism [31].

### 36.2.4.4 Therapy

The treatment of insulinomas is surgical. The extent of surgery depends on the localisation of tumour and preoperative localisation is crucial. The surgical treatment of other hormone-secreting types or non-functioning tumours can be considered; a size above 4 cm increases the chance of developing metasta ses. Medical treatment is the primary therapy for gastrinomas with proton pump inhibitors and in some cases the addition of an H2 receptor antagonist is needed.

### 36.2.5 Genetics

In 1997, Chandrasekharappa et al. identified the MEN1 gene on chromosome 11q13 and determined that the 9 kb MEN1 gene contains 10 exons coding for a 610 amino-acid protein, named menin [4]. Menin is a nuclear scaffold protein with regulating transcription, genome stability, cell division, and proliferation inter acting several protein partners. MEN1 is con sidered to act as a tumour suppressor gene. Most of the mutations result in a predicted loss of function of the protein, with a second hit at the somatic level resulting in no func tional protein in the tumour tissues, consis tent with a tumour suppressor mechanism [32, 33]. More than 1000 mutations have been reported, mainly frame-shift deletions and insertions, but nonsense, missense, splice-site alterations in-frame deletions and rarely large deletions are also known in the MEN1 gene [32]. No germline promoter mutations have been reported to date. There is no true muta

tion hotspots, although some changes were repeatedly reported in independent popula tions. Mutations are dispersed over the entire coding region.

Based on the Dutch MEN1 cohort, de Laat et al. developed a prediction rule predict ing the risk of MEN1 for patients with MEN1-related endocrine tumours with a family history and age at DNA testing also included in the model [34].

It is estimated that approximately 5–10% of patients with a clinical diagnosis of MEN1 are MEN1 gene-mutation negative because (i) mutations are located in MEN1 but not detected by the current genetic testing meth ods, (ii) other hereditary syndromes are pres ent (e.g., MEN4 due to CDKN1B mutations representing 3% of MEN1-like patients [12]) or (iii) the patient has a chance coexistence of two solid sporadic tumour (most frequently pituitary tumours and primary hyperparathy roidism) [35].

### 36.2.5.1 Genetic Testing and Clinical

### Screening

According to the guideline from 2012, genetic testing is advised in every patient with a clini cal diagnosis of MEN1, and also in patients at any age without fulfilling the diagnosis if MEN1 but with pHPT <30 years, having mul tinodular pHPT, gastrinomas or multiple pancreatic NETs [36]. Early diagnosis of MEN1 is obligatory to improve patient outcome, but the psychological and medical burden of screening at a young age should be considered. Current practice guidelines rec ommend DNA testing for the familial MEN1 mutation in children before the age of 5 years to establish an early diagnosis and periodic screening is recommended beginning in early childhood at the age of 5 years [37].

MEN1 mutation-positive children and adults need a life-long surveillance program including clinical visits to detect signs and symptoms of the disease, associated with reg ular biochemical and radiological screening in centres with experts and multidisciplinary teams. ### 36.3 Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia

### Type 2

### 36.3.1 Introduction

Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia Type 2 (MEN2) is an autosomal dominant heredi tary cancer syndrome caused by germline mutations in the REarranged during Transfection (RET) gene. MEN2 is divided into two entities such as MEN2A and MEN2B. MEN2A is subclassified into four subtypes such as classical MEN2A, MEN2A associated with cutaneous lichen amyloidosis (CLA), MEN2A associated with Hirschsprung’s disease, and familial medul lary thyroid cancer (FMTC). MEN2A repre sents approximately 95% of MEN2 syndromes.

### 36.3.2 Clinical Presentation

MEN2A is associated with medullary thyroid carcinoma, phaeochromocytoma and pri mary hyperparathyroidism. In the classical MEN2A syndrome, the prevalence of differ ent disorders includes medullary thyroid car cinoma 90%, phaeochromocytoma 30% and primary hyperparathyroidism 10% [48].

### 36

The clinical diagnosis of MEN2A can be established, when MTC and phaeochromocy toma with or without pHPT is present in the same patient or two or more tumours in mul tiple members of the same family. MTC is prevalent in almost all MEN2A patients and is usually the first clinical manifestation of the disease, preceded by C-cell hyperplasia. Depending on the exact location of the RET mutation within the gene, the mean age at diagnosis of the transition from normal thy roid morphology to C-cell hyperplasia ranges from 5 to 11 years. MTC in MEN2A is char acterised by multifocal, bilateral tumours typ ically located in the middle to upper part of the thyroid gland. The youngest patient with

MEN2A-related MTC reported in the litera ture was 10 months old [50].

Data from a nationwide retrospective cohort of MTC from Denmark showed that 15% of MEN2A patients were symptomatic at the age of diagnosis, while 85% were asymp tomatic. Their data highlighted the fact that patients with hereditary MTC diagnosed by screening without regional metastases, and with stage I, II or III disease, may have similar survival as the general population [51]. Disease-specific survival of MTC patients is strongly dependent on the age and stage at diagnosis. C-cell hyperplasia is a precursor of MTC, and in MEN2A and MEN2B, there is a progression from normal histology to C-cell hyperplasia and then to MTC. There is sig nificant phenotypic variability in the timing of MTC, onset and aggressiveness between carriers of MEN2 mutations in the same fam ily, especially in the moderate risk group. There is no real explanation for this phenom enon, and no marker to predict it at the indi vidual level.

The typical clinical features of phaeochro mocytoma are usually absent in MEN2associated adrenal tumours, as half of the patients are asymptomatic and only about one-third are hypertensive [52]. The majority of phaeochromocytomas in MEN2A patients are benign, bilateral, and contralateral phaeo chromocytomas usually develop within 10 years of the unilateral tumour [53].

Primary hyperparathyroidism is caused by parathyroid adenoma or hyperplasia. pHPT is usually diagnosed in the fourth decade of life. Carriers of codon 634 mutations are regarded as having the highest penetrance of pHPT. The youngest child reported in the literature was 5 years old, but it is rare for the disease to develop in childhood [54].

In MEN2A-CLA patients, dermatological lesions are typically located in the scapular region. They may be the first sign of the dis ease before the onset of MTC at an early age. In the subtype of MEN2A and CLA, the fre quency of phaeochromocytoma and pHPT appears to be the same as in classical MEN2A. In MEN2A with cutaneous lichen amyloidosis, the prevalence of different mani festations is medullary thyroid carcinoma


## PDF page 969 | printed page 973

95%, phaeochromocytoma 50% and primary hyperparathyroidism 15% [48]. The earliest age of onset reported in the literature is 3 years [55].

In MEN2A with Hirschsprung’s disease, medullary thyroid carcinoma occurs in 80%, phaeochromocytoma in 20% and primary hyperparathyroidism in 5% [48]. Almost all patients with MEN2A and Hirschsprung’s disease show signs of the abnormality soon after birth. Hirschsprung’s disease occurs in approximately 7% of MEN2A patients, and of patients with Hirschsprung’s disease, 2–5% have MEN2A [56–58].

FMTC is viewed as a milder phenotypic variant of MEN2A with medullary thyroid carcinoma alone, presenting with lower pene trance, later age of onset, and with less aggres sive disease than in classical MEN2A.

MEN2B is associated with medullary thy roid carcinoma, phaeochromocytoma and extra-endocrine manifestations, such as gan glioneuromas of the digestive tract (100% of cases) as well as musculoskeletal and ophthal mological abnormalities. Medullary thyroid carcinoma in MEN2B is the most prominent disease, and the tumour manifests an average 10 years earlier than in MEN2A [20]. It is pre ceded by C-cell hyperplasia followed by devel opment metastasises quickly to lymph nodes and to different organs (liver, lungs and bone brain). Clinical signs of gastrointestinal involvement include intermittent constipation and diarrhoea and, in some cases, urgent sur gery is required for bowel obstruction. Skeletal malformations are present in marfanoid body habitus (70% of cases), narrow long facies, pes cavus, pes excavatum, high-arched palate, sco liosis and slipped capital femoral epiphysis. In the international cohort, median age at first diagnosis of phaeochromocytoma in MEN2B patients was 24 years. Adrenalsparing surgery was done in 31 patients, three (10%) of 31 patients had long-term recur rence, while normal adrenal function was obtained in 16 (61%) of patients [49].

### 36.3.3 Diagnosis

The C-cells of the thyroid gland secrete calci tonin. The serum level of calcitonin indicates the size of the C-cell mass, and it is used as an important tumour marker for MTC. Serum calcitonin levels may be elevated in the absence of MTC in patients with various conditions, such as chronic renal failure, autoimmune thyroiditis, hyperparathyroidism or various neuroendocrine tumours, and in the presence of heterophilic antibodies. If there is a dis crepancy between the clinical signs and the low serum calcitonin levels found, a hook effect (an assay problem seen with sandwich immunoassays) must be ruled out. The paral lel measurement of the prohormone of calci tonin, procalcitonin and calcitonin resulting in a high ratio of procalcitonin to calcitonin suggests a worse outcome of the disease. The interpretation of calcitonin levels must be assay specific. In children, the reference range for calcitonin levels depends on age, and assay method data are available in different paediat ric age groups [59, 60]: <6 months of age < 40 ng/L, between 6 months and 3 years of age < 15 ng/L and over 3 years of age, the adult reference range is acceptable, for girls <5 ng/L, for boys <10 ng/L is acceptable for normal values. In individual cases, an increase in the calcitonin level after calcium or penta gastrin stimulation testing may help to deter mine the timing of prophylactic thyroidectomy in children with MEN2A, to diagnose or exclude the presence of MTC in thyroid nod ules or, for example, to detect progression after thyroidectomy. The production of carci noembryonic antigen is not specific for MTC, and no data for children are available [61]. In some cases, MTC can secrete other hormones in addition to calcitonin, e.g., ectopic ACTH production, leading to hypercortisolism, asso ciated with a poor outcome [62].

MEN2B is often caused by de novo muta tions; in these cases, the initial diagnosis is based on clinical features. In a study of 35 MEN2B children with de novo mutations, 22 presented with endocrine manifestations (phaeochromocytoma or space-occupying symptoms due to MTC) with a mean age of

It is crucially important to recognise the distinctive pheno type of MEN2B, as early diagnosis can help for early detection of elevated calcitonin and MTC, as well as parathyroid and adrenal dis ease [49].

### 36.3.4 Genetics

The RET proto-oncogene is located on chro mosome 10q11.2 and contains 21 exons. RET encodes a tyrosine kinase receptor with key roles in cells growth, differentiation and survival. This gene is widely expressed in var ious endocrine tissues, such as the C-cells of thyroid gland, adrenal medulla and pituitary as well as in neurons. The gene was discov ered to be an oncogene in 1985 [63]. The germline RET mutations associated with MEN2 result in a gain-of-function. In MEN2A, mutations are located mostly in exon 10 (codons 609, 611, 618, 620) or exon 11 (codon 630, 631, 634, 666), but mutations can also locate in the intracellular tyrosinekinase domain 1, exon 13 (codon 768, 790), exon 14 (codon 804), exon 15 (codon 891) and exon 16 (codon 912) [64]. A germline mutation at codon 634 of the RET gene can be detected in almost all patients of MEN2A and CLA. The most common RET muta tions in MEN2A and Hirschprung’s disease are at codons 620 and 618 [49, 65]. In typical clinical manifestations of MEN2A, the absence of a pathogenic germline mutation in RET gene is very rare. MEN2B is mainly caused by the germline M918T RET muta tion (>95% of cases) or by the A883F variant (<5%). An atypical form of MEN2B is known to present clinical signs of the disease only at the age of 20 to 30 years, and genetic analyses have shown duplicated and tandem RET germline mutations in these patients [64]. ### 36.3.4.1 Risk Categories of RET

### Mutations and Screening

Clinical aggressiveness of the MTC associa tion with mutations in RET genes are divided into three groups such as highest risk, high risk and moderate risk, according to the 2015 American Thyroid Association (ATA) guide line for the management of medullary thyroid carcinoma [67]. ### 36.5 Carney Complex (CNC)

### 36.5.1 Introduction

The first descriptions of CNC were reported in 1985. This syndrome including cardiac and cutaneous myxomas, myxoid mammary fibro adenomas, patchy skin pigmentation (lentigo and various types of naevi, often on the lips), primary pigmented nodular disease (PPNAD, in most cases with hyperfunction of the adre nal glands), pituitary disease (somatotroph/ lactotroph hyperplasia or adenoma with gigantism, acromegaly or biochemical growth hormone excess, rarely corticotroph ade noma) and testicular tumours (Sertoli cell tumours, Leydig cell tumours, adrenocortical residual tumours or a combination) [73]. Ovarian cysts have been described in twothird of CNC patients prospectively screened; four of the 18 patients were <20 years old and had uni- or bilateral cysts [74]. Rarely, these may develop into ovarian tumours, but ovar ian pathology is not among the diagnostic cri teria of the disease. Thyroid abnormalities, ranging from follicular hyperplasia and/or cystic changes to carcinoma, as well as, rarely, corticotroph pituitary adenoma, are clinical manifestations of CNC [75–77]. ### 36.5.3 Genetics

CNC is an autosomal dominant tumour syn drome. Approximately 70% of patients have a family history and the penetrance is almost complete. Thirty percent of CNC patients develop germline mutations de novo. In case of clinical diagnosis of CNC but without PRKAR1A mutations, the phenotype is less serious with later occurrence of manifesta tions [81]. The pathophysiology of the disease is based on dysregulation of the cAMP/pro tein kinase A signalling pathway and affects cell proliferation, differentiation and apopto sis. The PRKAR1A gene (protein kinase A regulatory subunit-1-alpha gene), previously known as the CNC1 gene, is a tumour sup pressor gene, located on chromosome 17q22–24 [82] and > 140 loss-of-function PRKAR1A mutations—nonsense, missense, small insertions or deletions or large dele tions—have been described. Large deletions result in earlier onset and more severe disease. All patients with a clinical diagnosis of CNC but no PRKAR1A mutation detected by Sanger sequencing should be tested for large deletions [83, 84]. Another gene defect, copy number variation in PRKACB (the catalytic subunit of protein kinase A at chromosome 1p31.1 region), was described in a 19-year-old women with acromegaly, lentigines and myxo mas [85]. Linkage studies identified a locus on the short arm of chromosome 2, but the iden tity of this putative ‘CNC2’ gene has remained unclear.

### 36.5.4 Clinical Presentation

There is a wide clinical variability even within family members. Based on the cumulative data of two retrospective and one prospective study, three-year follow-up cohort, frequency of endocrine manifestations in adults’ range between 45% and 69% for PPNAD, 10–19% for clinical acromegaly, 7.5–25% for thyroid tumours, 35–56% for LCCSCT and 8–14% for ovarian lesions [81, 86–88]. The main clinical presentation of Carney’s complex is PPNAD,

which pathologically appears as normal-sized or enlarged adrenal glands surrounded by an atrophic cortex with multiple brown and black nodules <1 cm in diameter. A pathognomonic hormonal finding is the paradoxical increase in glucocorticoid excretion as measured by 24-h urinary free cortisol on the second day of a high-dose dexamethasone trial. In most cases, imaging techniques do not provide use ful information on adrenal morphology, but in some cases, contrast-enhanced CT imaging can detect contour abnormalities and micronodular lesions of the adrenal cortex with a CT slice thickness of 3 mm or less. PPNAD can appear alone or part of other manifestations of the syndrome.

A transatlantic consortium studied the molecular genotype and clinical phenotype of 353 patients (221 females and 132 males, age 34 ± 19 years) who carried a germline PRKAR1A mutation or were diagnosed with CNC and/or primary pigmented nodular adrenocortical disease. Approximately half of the patients with CNC develop ACTHindependent Cushing’s syndrome. Cushing’s syndrome may be overt, cyclical, atypical or subclinical. About 20% of ACTHindependent Cushing syndrome caused by PPNAD develop in childhood. However, PPKAR1A mutations rarely detected in isolated PPNAD before age of 8 years [81]. Adrenocortical lesions are almost always benign, but there are a few reports in the lit erature of CNC and adrenocortical carci noma [89]. In patients with adrenal lesions larger than 3–4 cm, especially with androgen excess, one could consider the diagnosis of adrenocortical carcinoma. In these patients, adrenal expert surgeons should take into con sideration the debate over the use of open sur gery versus laparoscopy for small adrenal cancer.

The most common pituitary abnormalities involve the GH and prolactin axis. Pathologically, pituitary adenomas, pituitary hyperplasia or a combination of both have been described. GH secreting pituitary abnor malities can be multifocal. In most cases, almost 75% of CNC patients hormone results show abnormal GH axis dynamics, paradoxi

Only a few cases of corti cotroph adenomas, as part of the CNC, are known in the literature [76, 77]. One of the latter is a case report of a 15-year-old boy with an ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma and subsequent development of ACTHindependent Cushing’s syndrome, PPNAD, 5 years later.

Typically, the lesion is silent without hormone secretion, but due to increased P450 aromatase expression, clinical signs may include gynecomastia at prepubertal or peri pubertal age. In some cases, CNC is also associated with the development of Leydig cell tumours and residual adrenocortical tumours [91].

They can occur in any chamber of the heart and myxo mas associated with CNC are more aggressive than non-syndromic cases of cardiac myxo mas. They should be removed, but the recur rence rate is high. ### 36.5.5 Therapy and Surveillance

To date, there is no specific treatment approach for features of CNC, and management of the various manifestations are the same as in spo radic cases. PPAND is usually treated with bilateral adrenalectomy although there are reports of good results on unilateral adrenal ectomy. For GH-secreting adenomas and pituitary hyperplasia, surgery with selective adenomectomy or partial or complete hypophysectomy has been used. Dopamine analogues have good efficacy for hyperprolac tinaemia, while responsiveness to somatosta tin analogues is variable. Tumour progress of LCCSCT can reduce fertility in order to pre vent this, surgical removal is required in the event of progression. As most Leydig cell tumours are benign with a low tendency to relapse after surgical removal, testis-sparing surgery is recommended [94, 95]. Treatment of different thyroid lesions depends on the type of abnormality. Early detection and sur gical removal of thyroid cancer is important for patient survival. Cardiac myxoma is treated by surgery.

Prospective monitoring for the various manifestations of CNC using biochemical assessment, and imaging is recommended in patients clinically diagnosed with CNC or in asymptomatic subjects with PRKAR1A gene mutations [91, 96]. Clinical surveillance depends on age. Early diagnosis associated

with better outcome, but annually from 6 months of age (twice yearly in those with a history of excised myxoma), screening should be started as soon as the diagnosis of CNC is made. Boys should undergo regular ultra sound scans of the testicles. Abnormal growth rate or puberty development are indications for further investigations. In adolescents and adults, annual or biannual echocardiography (based on history of previous myxoma), annual testicular and thyroid ultrasound and measurement of 24-h urinary free cortisol and serum IGF-1 levels are required. Females should undergo pelvic ultrasound, with repeat imaging depending on findings [87, 91].

### 36.6 Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia

### Type 4

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 4 (MEN4) was first described in 2006 [97]. It is a MEN1like syndrome with a different genetic back ground. Inactivating heterozygous germline mutations in the CDKN1B gene, coding for the cell cycle inhibitor p27, are responsible for the development of the syndrome, which is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait. The gene is located on chromosome 12q13 and functions as a tumour suppressor gene. Only a small number of patients have been identified with this syndrome, and the penetrance is not known. The clinical features of the syndrome are similar to MEN1, and the exact diagnosis can only be made by genetic analysis. Hyperparathyroidism and pituitary tumours are the most common manifestations of MEN4. Hyperparathyroidism in MEN4 occurs in approximately 75% of adult patients and usually presents as a uniglandular lesion. Pituitary tumours, most commonly somato trophinomas and prolactinomas, are known to occur in about 40% of adult MEN4 patients, and are less aggressive and present later than in MEN1. In apparently unaffected CDKN1B carriers, non-functioning pituitary adenomas are the most common pituitary manifestations of the disease [84, 98]. Mutations in the CDKN1B gene are rare in paediatric patients with pituitary adenomas,


## PDF page 976 | printed page 980

but a childhood-onset GH excess and a few cases of corticotophinomas have been described [84, 99–101]. All patients with clini cal features typical of MEN1 but without detectable MEN1 mutations should undergo genetic screening for CDKN1B [102]. In rare cases, other cell cycle inhibitor mutations in CDKN1A (coding for p21), CDKN2B (p15) or CDKN2C (p18) have also been seen in MEN1-like syndromes [103].

### 36.7 Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia

### Type 5

Loss-of-function heterozygous mutations have been described in the MAX gene (MYCassociated factor X gene) at chromosome 14q23.3 in patients with phaeochromocyto mas. More recently, hyperparathyroidism and pituitary adenomas have also been associated with this gene and the term multiple endo crine neoplasia type 5 has been suggested. The MAX protein is an essential part of the MYC- MAX-MXD1 network, which plays a crucial function in regulating cell proliferation, dif ferentiation and apoptosis. Phaeochromocytomas are the most common manifestation of MEN5, presenting as bilat eral multifocal lesions of the adrenal medulla, but without typical histopathological features to distinguish them from sporadic phaeochromocytomas [102]. MAX immuno histochemistry is available but is of limited value in the diagnosis of MEN5 syndrome due to poor sensitivity and specificity [102]. Pituitary tumours may be present in early childhood or adolescence [104]. MAX gene mutations have also been associated to renal oncocytoma, lung cancer, neuroblastoma, ganglioneuroma, ganglioneuroblastoma, extra-adrenal sympathetic paraganglioma or mixed phaeochromocytoma and ganglioneu roma [21, 105]. All individuals with patho genic mutations in the MAX gene should have a baseline evaluation for paraganglioma, pitu itary and parathyroid tumours with biochemi cal and imaging evaluation, but formalised screening details are not yet established [106].

### 36.8 Hereditary Paraganglioma-

### Phaeochromocytoma

### 36.8.1 Introduction

Hereditary paraganglioma/phaeochromocy toma (PPGL) is an autosomal dominantly inherited syndrome characterised by the development of sympathetic and parasympa thetic nervous system and adrenal medulla tumours. The development of PPGLs is due to an increasing list of germline mutations (see below). The succinate dehydrogenase genes—most commonly mutated in patients with PPGL—encode the subunits of the mitochondrial succinate dehydrogenase enzyme, a tricarboxylic acid cycle enzyme that converts succinate to fumarate in the cit ric acid cycle and also participates in the elec tron transport chain. Further roles have also been described regarding tumorigenesis, such as in angiogenesis and in DNA methylation [107]. Defect in enzyme mechanism causes cellular accumulation of succinate known to be an important metabolite in tumorigenesis an oncometabolite. Germline mutations in different genes cause the five different types of succinate dehydrogenase that predispose to the development of one type of hereditary PPGL: PGL1 (SDHD), where the inheritance follows an maternally imprinted pattern, which means that PPGLs develop in subjects who inherited the gene from their father, while if the gene was inherited from the mother the disease does not develop, but the gene, of course, can be transmitted to offspring); PGL2 (SDHAF2); PGL3 (SDHC); PGL4 (SDHB) and PGL5 (SDHA). Mutations in the SDHD and SDHAF2 genes cause clinical manifestations only in the case of paternal inheritance. SDH-related tumours, collec tively known as SDHx, manifest in different organs as phaeochromocytomas from the adrenal medulla, paragangliomas from sym pathetic and parasympathetic nervous sys tem, gastrointestinal stromal tumours (GIST), clear cell renal cell carcinoma, pituitary tumours and others.

### 36.8.2 Clinical Presentations

### 36.8.2.1 Phaeochromocytomas

### and Paragangliomas

Paragangliomas of sympathetic origin are mainly located in the lower mediastinum, abdomen and pelvis, and may secrete cate cholamines, whereas those of parasympa thetic origin are typically located in the skull base, neck and upper mediastinum and are usually non-functional. The symptoms of PPGLs are caused by hypersecretion of epi nephrine (adrenalin), norepinephrine (nor adrenalin) and dopamine. In children, typical symptoms are hypertension, sweating, tachy cardia or palpitations, headache and symp toms of tumour pressure. Complications of catecholamine hypersecretion include hyper tensive crises, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, pancreatitis, constipation, intestinal pseudoobstruction, stroke and seizures. Symptoms of paragangliomas include hearing loss, pul satile tinnitus and symptoms of a mass effect on the neck [20]. In a large cohort of 748 patients with PPGLs, including 95 with first presentation in childhood, differences in pre sentation and genetic background of PPGLs between children and adults were analysed [108]. Data showed that children had a higher prevalence than adults of hereditary (80.4% vs. 52.6%), extra-adrenal (66.3% vs. 35.1%), multifocal (32.6% vs. 13.5%), metastatic (49.5% vs. 29.1%) and recurrent (29.5% vs. 14.2%) PPGLs. The hereditary form was ana lysed by genetic testing of 14 tumour suscepti bility genes. Mutations of the SDHB gene were detected in approximately 40% (86% metastatic and 19% recurrent) and mutations of SDHD in approximately 10% (33% meta static, 66% recurrent) of PPGLs diagnosed in childhood. No SDHA and SDHC mutations were detected in this age group. In a review of the literature up to 2018, SDHB gene altera tions were present in 73.3%, SDHC in 2.9% and SDHD in 23.8% of reported paediatric cases with SDHx mutations [109]. A third of the tumours were multifocal and a quarter were malignant.

In adults, pituitary tumours often pres ent as macroadenomas with aggressive behav iour as somatotrophinomas, prolactinomas, non-functioning pituitary adenomas or rarely corticotrophinomas [111, 112]. ### 36.8.3 Genetics

There are an increasing number of genes asso ciated with PPGL. Heterozygous loss-of-function germline mutations in SDHx genes are responsible for approximately 20% of cases of PPGLs, and can also cause other SDHx-related tumours. There are nonsense, missense, splice site, frameshift and in a small percentage copy number variations of SDHx genes causing hereditary PPGLs [117]. The mutations are inherited in an autosomal dom inant pattern, except for MDH2 which is bial lelic, but penetrance for PPGL of the clinical manifestations has a great variability (between 8 and 37% for SDHB, and 38% and 64% for SDHD in different report of the literature) [118]. The penetrance for pituitary adenomas is very low, <1% [111].

### 36.8.4 Diagnosis

Urinary or plasma free metanephrines analy sis is the best test to detect tumours hormone

Age-specific paediatric ref erence intervals for plasma free normetaneph rine, metanephrine, 3-methoxytyramine and 3-O-methyldopa published in 2019 are useful in interpreting hormone results [119]. Children and adolescences with PPGLs have to be managed by multidisciplinary

### 36.9 von Hippel-Lindau Disease

Von Hippel-Lindau disease (VHL) is an autosomal dominant condition with a pene trance of a prevalence of 1/36,000 and pen etrance up to 87% at the age of 65 years. The VHL tumour suppressor gene, located on chromosome 3p25–26, codes for a protein, which is an E3 ligase for ubiquitination of hypoxia inducible transcription factors (HIFs). Lack of VHL protein leads to reduced degradation of HIF with effects on angiogenesis and tumour formation. The majority of VHL cases are familial with ~20% of mutations occur de novo. VHL manifestations include retinal, cerebellar, and medullary haemangioblastomas, renal cell carcinomas, renal cysts, pancreatic cysts, and neuroendocrine tumours, phaeochro mocytomas, endolymphatic sac tumours, and cysts in the epididymis and road uterine ligament. In a screening study, 70% of chil dren and adolescents had at least one tumour and 60% had two. The mean age at first pre sentation was found to be at 12 years, but prospective studies will probably see this age to be lower in future studies. Concentrating on endocrine manifestations, the prevalence of pancreatic cysts (20%) and phaeochro mocytomas (14%) suggest that imaging (ultrasound, MRI) examination of the abdo men could be started above the age of 5 years, and not later than 14 years [122].

Only a few cases are also known with 3PAs and VHL gene defects: one of them is a paedi atric case, but it is unclear if this could be a coincidence or if the VHL mutation plays a role in the pathogenesis of the pituitary tumour [111, 123].

### 36.10 DICER1 Syndrome

### 36.10.1 Introduction

Germline loss-of-function mutations in DICER1 are associated with a pleiotropic tumour susceptibility syndrome, character ised by pleuropulmonary blastoma, cystic nephroma and other rare entities and also non-neoplastic condition as multinodular goitre seems to be feature of the disease. Many of the disorders occur in childhood, such as the first described and most characteristic tumour, familial pleuropulmonary blastoma with a typical age of onset in childhood of <6 years, often presenting in first few months of life. Other features of the disease, such as various tumours and dysplasias, also often develop in childhood and adolescence: 80% of cases are familial and 20% because of de novo mutations [124]. The penetrance of the dis ease is incomplete, most individuals with a pathogenic germline mutation of DICER1 are healthy, only one-third of DICER1 mutation carriers develop a DICER1 correlated tumour during their lifetime [125, 126]. Pathogenic germline DICER1 variants are estimated to occur in the general population in 1/10,600 people [127].

### 36

### 36.10.2 Genetics

The DICER1 gene encodes an RNA endonu clease (member of the ribonuclease III fam ily), involved in the generation of microRNAs which modulate post-transcriptional gene expression. In DICER1 syndrome-associated neoplasms—following the Knudson two-hit hypothesis—heterozygous germline loss-offunction DICER1 mutations are associated with somatic missense mutations usually at one of the five hotspot codons and result in hypomorphic DICER1 RNase IIIb function [128]. Penetrance is incomplete and variable for the different tumour types. Analysis of data from three large cohorts of 207 patients

carrying germline pathogenic variants in DICER1 showed that by age 10 years of age, 5.3% of the non-proband carriers (n = 102) and by age 50 years, 19.3% of them had devel oped a neoplasm [129].

### 36.10.4 Diagnosis

The diagnosis of DICER1 is established by identification of a heterozygous germline DICER1 pathogenic variant that is known or suspected to cause loss-of-function. Molecular genetic testing approaches can include a com bination of gene-targeted testing (single-gene testing and multigene panel) and comprehen sive genomic testing (exome sequencing, exome array and genome sequencing) depend ing on the phenotype [124].

Testing of at-risk newborns (before age 4 months) for the family-specific pathogenic variant is recommended. If a germline DICER1 pathogenic variant has been identi fied in an affected family member, it is reason able to offer molecular genetic testing to at-risk relatives of all ages to clarify their genetic status and to provide recommenda tions for age-appropriate surveillance and early intervention. In rare instances, large lung cysts may cause respiratory distress in newborns, and thus, a third-trimester ultra sound is recommended for pregnancies in which the foetus is at risk for a DICER1 pathogenic variant. If lung cysts are identi fied, consultation with specialists in high-risk obstetrics and fetal medicine is recommended [124].

### 36

### 36.10.5 Therapy

The treatment of DICER1-related tumours depends on the type and stage of the tumour. Several of these tumours, such as pleuroblas toma and cystic nephroma, have an initial cys tic form and later progress to a high-grade, more malignant tumour. Early diagnosis can provide the opportunity to cure the tumour in its most curable form [141].

Treatment of DICER1-associated tumours includes surgical resection with or without chemotherapy, in some cases com bined with radiotherapy [141]. For pituitary blastoma, near or total resection of the tumour may be sufficient (5/9 children with long-term survival) [137]. In the case of pineo

blastoma, radiotherapy to the primary site and adjuvant therapy may be the treatment of choice.

### 36.10.6 Surveillance

If a pathogenic DICER1 germline mutation is detected in first-degree relatives, genetic screening of newborns before the age of 4 months is recommended. Recommended surveillance strategies for DICER1-associated diseases in patients with DICER1 germline mutations were published in 2018 [130]. According to this recommendation, a first chest CT is recommended between 3 and 6 months of age and a second chest CT between 2.5 and 3 years of age. Intermittent chest radiographs are also recommended every 6 months until 8 years of age and annu ally from 8 to 12 years of age. There is no con sensus on the need for regular brain imaging by MRI, but symptoms of intracranial pathol ogy or hormonal abnormalities require urgent investigation for pituitary blastoma or pineo blastoma. Abdominal and pelvic ultrasound is recommended twice a year from birth to 8 years of age, and annually thereafter to screen for cystic nephroma, kidney or sex cord tumours. Thyroid ultrasound should be started at 8 years of age and repeated every 3 years thereafter. Symptoms of eye and nasal tumours and gastrointestinal polyps require further urgent investigations.

### 36.11 Familial Isolated Pituitary

### Adenoma

### 36.11.1 Introduction

Pituitary adenomas (PitNETs) develop from hormone-secreting differentiated pituitary cells. They are usually benign with clinical symptoms of hormone excess and mass effects causing visual disturbances and hypopituita rism. There is atypical age distribution of pae diatric pituitary adenoma subtypes, with


## PDF page 983 | printed page 987

corticotrophinomas being the most common in early childhood and prolactinomas and somatotrophinomas being the most common subtypes in later adolescence [142]. Most ade nomas are microadenomas, macroadenomas being less common in childhood and are mainly somatotrophinomas or prolactinomas. Clinically, non-functioning pituitary adeno mas are less commonly detected in children and adolescents than in adults, accounting for only 5–10.5% of adenomas, while small inci dentalomas are also present in the paediatric population, again with lower prevalence than in adults. Paediatric pituitary adenomas can be rapidly growing and invasive in a small per centage of the cases [143].

Hereditary form of pituitary adenomas is more common in children than in adults. They may occur as part of the clinical manifesta tions of a syndromic disease (as discussed above) or as an isolated form without other endocrine or non-endocrine manifestations, known as familial isolated pituitary adenoma (FIPA). FIPA is characterised by the presence of pituitary adenomas in at least two family members; however, due to low penetrance, simplex cases are common. Familial cases, based on the pituitary tumour type occurring in the family, can be divided into homoge neous and heterogeneous forms. In the homo geneous form, all family members have the same type of pituitary adenomas, whereas in the heterogeneous form, different subtypes of adenomas are present in the affected family members. While this type of characterisation would be logical, the various syndrome some times spans both types of presentations. Based on the genetic results, currently two genes have been firmly established for FIPA: loss-of-function mutations in AIP or duplica tion in GPR101 causing X-linked acrogigan tism (X-LAG) [144, 145].

### 36.11.2 Clinical Presentation

FIPA has a clinical presentation in childhood and adolescence and is very distinctive, some what matching the heterogeneous genetic background. By far, the most common pitu

itary tumour type in childhood and adoles cence FIPA is GH-secreting adenomas, often manifest with tall stature or gigantism. The definition of gigantism is arbitrary. Theoretically, any GH-secreting adenoma occurring before epiphysis closure could be categorised as gigantism, independent of height parameters; sometimes, height velocity data can be used, if available. In some publica tions, the definition of gigantism in patients with completed growth is established when the patient’s height is >2 standard deviations above mid-parental height/centile, or 3 stan dard deviations above the country-specific average height. The development of extreme tall stature requires prolonged GH excess, either because the disease started well before puberty or because the tumour itself damages gonadotroph cell function by pressure on gonadotroph cells and/or LH/FSH suppres sion due to coexisting hyperprolactinaemia, which itself could be either due to active secre tion of a tumour or due to stalk effect. We need to be well aware that while GH excess is the most common cause of extreme tall stat ure, other diseases can also result in a similar excess heights, such as Marfan syndrome or disease associated with activating mutations of the natriuretic C pathway [146, 147]. Data from an international study cohort of 153 non-syndromic patients diagnosed with pitu itary gigantism, 63 had AIP mutations (41.2%) and 78 patients (51%) were negative for both AIP mutations and GPR101 duplication. Twelve patients (10 females, 2 males) were identified with GPR101 duplication (also germline or somatic), representing 7.8% of the total cohort [148].

### 36.11.2.1 X-LAG

Patients with X-LAG have early-onset gigan tism [149]; patients are diagnosed before the age of 5 years, but growth acceleration usually begins in the first year of life [144, 145], with cases identified already in utero. Data from patients with a GPR101 duplication from an international cohort showed that X-LAG patients were generally born at term with nor mal birth length and weight. All patients showed accelerated growth from the age of 7

months, with a median age at onset of 1.9 years (range 1.1–2.4 years) [148]. Most cases of X-LAG are sporadic, but there are a few known cases published with mother-toson transmission. Only girls have been identi fied with the sporadic form of X-LAG associated with germline mutations, while sporadic male cases harbour the mutation in a mosaic form. No difference in clinical pheno type is observed between patients with germ line and somatic mosaic duplications of the GPR101 gene [144, 145, 148, 150]. In most cases, the disease presents as a tumour but in about 20–25% of cases as pituitary hyperpla sia. Over 80% of cases have associated hyper prolactinaemia. In addition to rapid growth, other features of growth hormone excess, oth erwise rarely observed in children with somatotrophinomas, include prominent signs of disproportional acral enlargement and facial changes (facial coarsening, prominent mandible), excess appetite, fasting hyperinsu linemia (1/third of cases) and acanthosis nigricans (1/fifth of cases). ### 36

### 36.11.2.2 AIP Mutation Positive Cases

These patients typically present with growth hormone or prolactin-secreting adenomas in adolescence; 65% of AIP mutation positive patients with pituitary adenomas develop symptoms before the age of 18 years, but ear lier and later manifestations have been described, with youngest cases presenting with apoplexy due to a macroadenoma at age 4 years. A family history can be present only in about half of the probands, due to incom plete penetrance (20–25%). Therefore, germ line mutations of the AIP gene have also been identified in patients with seemingly sporadically-occurring pituitary adenomas [151–153]. Only one case has been described with an established de novo mutation, and somatic AIP mutations have not been identi fied to date. Pituitary adenomas are typically

large, invasive in nature and aggressive in behaviour [151, 154], with susceptibility to pituitary apoplexy [151]. Rarely hyperplasia have been described next to an adenoma, and multiple tumours can occur in the same patient.

Gene carriers with normal biochemistry and MRI findings at the age around 30 years do not develop the disease later [150, 155]. There is a male predominance in AIP mutation-positive patients, although taller stature in males may prompt genetic testing in some cases. Via prospective screening, several young-onset cases have been identified and treated earlier than clinical changes would allow, resulting in better outcomes. Furthermore, carriers are recognised with small clinically non-functioning lesions which often behave like incidentalomas.

### 36.11.2.3 FIPA Without AIP or GPR101

### Mutations

The phenotype of AIP and GPR101 mutationnegative [148] patients are different, with pre sentations occurring typically in adulthood around the fourth decade. The distribution of AIP mutation versus AIP-negative FIPA patients according to age at onset of the dis ease shows 9% versus 3% between 0 and 9 years of age and 56% versus 27% between 10 and 19 years of age [150, 155]. The pituitary tumour type is also different with a more var ied phenotype than the strict GH/PRL excess in GPR101 and AIP-related cases. In the FIPA consortium cohort overall, acromegaly is the most common manifestation closely fol lowed by prolactinomas, with clinically nonfunctioning tumours showing a lower prevalence and corticotroph adenomas seen rarely; there were no familiar TSHomas in this cohort. Regarding the AIP-negative FIPA patients with first symptoms at ≤19 years, 47% have prolactinomas, 37% show GH excess, while 9% have NFPA and 7% Cushing’s.

### 36.11.3 Diagnosis

Diagnosis in FIPA may be based on clinical manifestations, similar to sporadic pituitary tumours, or on clinical screening test results.

A diagnosis based on clinical features can be striking, due to, for example, the age of the patient in X-LAG cases or the presentation can be characteristic, such as a child with abnormal tall stature and pituitary apoplexy. Screening in carrier family members can iden tify disease in cases where certain existing symptoms were not recognised as being due to a pituitary tumour [156] or it could be wellbefore clinical manifestations [155] or even in utero [157].

Recognising the familial nature of these patients can be challenging. In the past, it was not recognised that isolated pituitary ade noma could be a genetic disease, despite a clear description of familial pituitary ade noma from the end of the nineteenth century, and even by Harvey Cushing [158]. While in cases with other family members having such disease, it is relatively straight forward, low penetrance leads to seemingly simplex cases (AIP) or there are de novo mutations (XLAG). In AIP/GPR101 negative families, where pen etrance is even lower than in FIPA families, the diagnosis of FIPA can only be set up when the second person is affected, and this often does not happen until middle age.

### 36.11.4 Genetics

AIP behaves as a tumour suppressor gene in the pituitary, with heterozygous germline lossof-function mutations. The AIP protein acts as a cochaperone to numerous proteins. The latest studies suggest that association with protein kinase C delta, caspase 3 and the intracellular part of the RET protein creates a complex which upregulates p53 via PIT1 and to balance somatotroph growth or apoptosis [159]. Lack of AIP disrupts this balance and pushes towards pituitary tumour develop ment.

Genetic testing for the AIP mutation is indicated in patients, with or without a family history, who have childhood-onset somato troph or lactotroph pituitary of any size, or older patients up till 30 years with macroade noma [22, 160]. Most of the AIP mutations can be identified with Sanger sequencing, but there are a few cases of whole gene or exon

deletions, so MLPA or next-generation sequencing should also be applied. There are a few hotspots in the gene (R304Stop, R81Stop, R271W), but no reliable genotypephenotype correlation has been identified.

X-LAG is due to a germline or somatic/ mosaic duplications of the GPR101 gene. A microduplication containing four proteincoding genes at chromosome X at 26.3 is seen in most patients (heterozygous in females and hemizygous in males), but smaller duplica tions have also been described. Only one of these genes, GPR101, which encodes an orphan G-protein-coupled receptor, is overex pressed in the patient’s pituitary disease [149]. The disease is caused by a disruption of the normal regulation of this gene associated with a new regulatory scenario affecting the topologically-associating domain (TAD) sur rounding this gene; therefore, X-LAG repre sents the first endocrine TADopathy [161]. Penetrance of X-LAG appears to be 100% as all females with heterozygous and all males with hemizygous GPR101 duplications mani fest the disease [84, 145]. No difference was observed between individuals with a duplica tion in GPR101 only and those with a larger duplication involving three more genes. As the clinical picture is so characteristic, testing is indicated in patients with increased growth velocity under the age of 5 years. Genetic test ing using standard comparative genomic hybridisation (CGH) array, and in negative cases, copy number variation digital droplet PCR for GPR101 or high-density array-CGH, should be used to determine the genetic back ground of the disease. In germline mutationnegative patients with the characteristic phenotype, genetic analysis of pituitary ade noma tissue for GPR101 mutations should be performed.

The first-line treatment for AIP-positive, AIPnegative and X-LAG FIPA is surgery. Patients with AIP-positive FIPA and X-LAG often require multimodal approaches such as sur gery, re-operation combined with radiother apy and medical therapy (somatostatin

AIP mutation-positive patients—There is a poor response to first-generation somatosta tin analogues, but second-generation soma tostatin analogues show good results in some (but not all) AIP mutation-positive patients [162, 163]. Prolactinomas show reasonable responsiveness to cabergoline, while somato troph adenomas respond poorly to firstgeneration somatostatin analogues. Response to pegvisomant is excellent, if applied in appropriate doses, and this could be applied early in management to reduce height velocity and improve symptoms, or while waiting the effect of other therapies. Patients respond well to radiotherapy.

X-LAG patients—In the case of pituitary hyperplasia, total hypophysectomy was per formed in the past, but combination medical treatment can now successfully treat these patients [164–166]. Patients with pituitary tumours have surgery as first-line treatment with somatostatin analogues, pegvisomant and radiotherapy as further options.

### 36.11.6 Screening

Genetic screening for family members should start for AIP mutations as soon as the family agree, ideally before the age of 4 years [150]. For carriers, regular physical examination, determination of growth velocity according to age and sex using pop ulation-based data, should be established. Annual biochemical testing (GH, IGF-1, prolactin) should be performed no later from 10 years of age. MRI imaging of the pituitary gland is recommended at 10 years of age and then every 5 years until 30 years of age [22, 150, 160]. Preimplantation genetic testing is available for families wising to consider that.

Based on the data available, in females, the disease is de novo. Future siblings may have slightly higher risk of disease due to gonadal

mosaicism in one of the parents, while for off spring, preimplantation and prenatal genetic testing is available. For males with an inher ited mutation, preimplantation and prenatal genetic testing is available, while in mosaic cases, the presence of the mutation in the gonad should be considered.

### 36.12 Summary

Identifying a genetic condition in a child or young person has numerous negative conse qunces. In addition to the disease burden from potentially multiple tumours and other mani festations, the diagnosis and carrier status puts a high psychological burden on the patient and their families. There could be issues regarding secrecy, guilt, inappropriate engagement with medical services, or with behaviour (such as avoiding having children). The relationship between health-care provid ers and patients and their families are tested by screening fatigue, difficulties around transi tion to adult services, the burden of ionising imaging, repeated surgery, side effects and complications of surgical, medical or radia tion treatment. Surveillance in childhood should be planned to balance the need for early disease detection for the particular genetic disorder and should minimise, as much as possible, the strain on the families. It is clear that a multidisciplinary team with all the appropriate medical, surgical, radiother apy and imaging specialists, as well as genetic services and nursing support, is key for the best possible outcome. The role of patient support organisations is also key to optimise the patient journey.
