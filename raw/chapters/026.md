### 26.1 Introduction and Background

The European Society for Pediatric Endocrinol ogy (ESPE) and the Pediatric Endocrine Society (PES) replaced the terms intersex, hermaphro ditism, pseudohermaphroditism, sex errors of the body, and ambiguous genitalia with an umbrella term, disorders of sex development (DSD) in 2006. DSD is defined as “congenital conditions in which development of chromo somal, gonadal, or anatomical sex is atypical” [1]. Most patients and professionals supported the nomenclature change as the prevailing ter minologies were confusing to patients and their medical providers and considered derogatory by some. DSDs are rare conditions, and the lack of specific medical codes precludes the collection of accurate population statistics for DSD. How ever, the estimated incidence is 0.1–0.5% of live births [2]. Approximately one in 4000 infants are born with atypical external genitalia and will receive a DSD diagnosis [1].

The current genetic-based classification system of DSD highlights the importance of chromosomal analysis in diagnosing and managing individuals with DSD. Since its inception, the term DSD has been accepted and used widely by the medical profession; however, critics have raised concerns because of the negative connotations associated with the words “disorder” and “sex” [3, 4]. Some professionals and organizations define “DSD” as Differences of Sexual Development, hence the two terms used in the title of this chapter.

### 26.2 Purpose

The aims of this chapter are to (1) provide a concise update on the mechanisms controlling normal and abnormal sexual differentiation;

(2) share a protocol used for the practical management of infants born with DSD; (3) provide information to guide the physician when making a DSD diagnosis and designing its medical management; (4) provide new behavioral information on DSD; and (5) high light current differences of opinions about the care of infants born with DSD.

Contrary to previous belief, ovarian development is not a passive or default pro cess. Ovarian development appears to depend on the suppression of testis-inducing genes and activation of ovary-inducing genes. ### 26.4 The Paired Internal Ducts:

### Wolffian and Müllerian Ducts

### 26.4.1 Wolffian Ducts

In 46, XY males, local testosterone produc tion by the Leydig cells helps the differentia tion of the Wolffian duct into the epididymis,

vas deferens, seminal vesicles, and ejaculatory ducts [34, 35]. Human chorionic gonadotro pin (hCG) initially regulates testosterone pro duction [36]. Additionally, AMH plays a vital role in developing internal ducts. The function of AMH is highlighted in agonadal genetic males, where Müllerian ducts persist because AMH is not produced [34, 37]. Typically, AMH is produced by the Sertoli cells. Hence, AMH is a marker of Sertoli cell function in patients with DSDs [38].

### 26.4.2 Müllerian Ducts

Müllerian duct differentiation progresses without AMH and develops the fallopian tubes, uterus, cervix, and upper third of the vagina. The transcription factor WNT4 plays an essential role in Müllerian duct differentia tion and ovarian function during fetal life. Female WNT4 knock-out mice fail to develop Müllerian structures and lose oocytes [30]. Further, they manifest hypersecretion of ovar ian androgens derived from Leydig cell pre cursors in the interstitium [39]. Thus, it appears that WNT4 suppresses androgen pro duction by Leydig cells’ precursors in the ova ry’s interstitium and is a regulator of Müllerian duct development.

### 26.4.3 The Bipotential External

### Genitalia

The primordial structures of the male and female external genitalia are homologous, and the external genitalia of genetic males cannot be distinguished from genetic females at 8 weeks of life. The genital tubercle becomes either the clitoris or the penis. The urethral folds become the labia minora, the clitoral hood, the skin covering the penis, and the foreskin. Finally, the labioscrotal folds or gen ital swellings become the labia majora or fuse together to become the scrotum. The future development and differentiation of the bipo tential external genitalia from this homolo gous state into male structures depend on the presence of androgens. Female-appearing

### 26.4.4 Male External Genitalia

The development of male external genitalia depends on the adequacy of testosterone (T) and 5-α (alpha)—dihydrotestosterone (5α (alpha)-DHT) production, as well as func tional androgen receptors (AR). The testes form by 6–7 weeks of fetal life. The Leydig cells are stimulated by placental hCG in the first trimester [36] and fetal pituitary LH in the second trimester [40]. Male external geni tal differentiation is complete by 12 weeks; additional penile enlargement in the second trimester depends on Leydig cell stimulation by LH from the fetal pituitary gland.

Complete masculinization of external male genitalia requires the conversion of T to 5α (alpha) DHT; the enzyme responsible for this conversion is 5α (alpha)-reductase, which is present in genital tissue. 46, XY infants with 5α (alpha)-reductase deficiency have normally functioning testes; the predominantly female phenotype in early childhood is due to the defec tive conversion of T to 5α (alpha) DHT during early fetal life. However, 5-α (alpha) DHT is critical for normal external male genital differ entiation in fetal life and the virilization of males during puberty [41–43]. Therefore, gender reas signment to males usually occurs in puberty in many societies where rates of 5α (alpha)-reduc tase deficiency are high. Cultural practices and preferences play another important role in gen der reassignment. In many of these cultures, male identity is valued over female identity. Historically, these cultures expect a change from female to male and have even codified such expectations in their language [44].

### 26.4.5 Female External Genitalia

Female external genitalia will be normal except when exposed to androgens in early

fetal life. Androgens may be derived from (1) the mother (ingested or endogenously pro duced); (2) the fetus (Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia (CAH)); (3) due to homozygous or heterozygous mutations in the placental aromatase gene, which leads to a failure to convert maternal androgens to estrogens, with virilization of both the mother and the fetus during the pregnancy; (4) ovotesticular DSD; (5) mixed gonadal dysgenesis [45–47]. The vir ilization degree depends on the quantity, tim ing, and actions of androgens to which the 46, XX fetus is exposed. Virilization ranges from clitoromegaly to severe posterior fusion of labia majora, absence of the labia minora and vaginal orifice, or a urogenital sinus with a large phallic-appearing clitoris that resembles hypospadias. Rarely, extreme virilization causes complete labioscrotal fusion and a “penile urethra” (Prader stage 5). In the last example, the external genitalia appears typical male. Therefore, the only clue to the possibility of a genetic female is the absence of palpable gonads in an individual with cryptorchidism.

### 26.6.1 Medical History

From the outset, the following simple ques tions guide the physician with the differential diagnosis and what tests to order: 1. Is the infant a genetic female (46, XX)

exposed to fetal androgens (i.e., 46, XX DSD)? 2. Is the infant an undervirilized genetic male

(46, XY) due to underproduction or decreased action of androgens (i.e., 46, XY DSD)? 3. Does the infant have a complex sex chro

mosome disorder (such as ovotesticular DSD in which 80% of patients have a 46, XX karyotype, or mixed gonadal dysgen esis, 45, X/46, XY)? 4. Is the genital defect the result of a birth

defect in structures that distort the genita lia (i.e., epispadias, cloacal exstrophy, or aphallia)?

A thorough history often provides essential information that helps in the diagnosis. Spe cial attention is given to the history of mater nal drug or medication use (anabolic steroids or androgens in oral contraceptives), general health and endocrine status (maternal hirsut ism or virilization, including virilization dur ing the pregnancy), family history (such as infertile non-menstruating maternal female relatives with scant or absent pubic and axil lary hair, which suggests androgen insensitiv ity with an X-linked recessive inheritance pattern) [52], and consanguinity in the parents (for autosomal recessively inherited condi tions such as 5α-reductase deficiency). Ante natal data such as ultrasound examinations and genetic studies obtained from chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis are invalu able in the diagnostic process. For example, a discordance between fetal karyotype (46, XY) and antenatal ultrasound genital findings (no phallus) may provide early evidence of andro gen resistance or aphallia.

### 26.6.2 Physical Exam

A gonad located in the inguinal canal by either manual palpation or ultrasound exam is highly informative. In nearly all cases, an external gonad is a testis. In the absence of an external gonad, the genetic sex of the infant cannot be identified by inspection if the infant has (1) 46, XY DSD with incomplete virilization, (2) 46, XX DSD with excess virilization, (3) ovotes ticular DSD, or (4) mixed gonadal dysgenesis.

It is best to avoid using absolute terms such as penis or scrotum until the diagnostic studies are completed. Instead, the phallic structure (clitoris or penis) is measured and examined for the presence of chordee, a downward curving phallus due to a shortened ventral surface. The phallic measurement may not be easy because an erectile mass is sometimes buried in pubic fat and is curved. A tape measure placed on the point of origin on the pubic ramus along the dorsum to the “erectile mass” gives a reason able estimate of length [53, 54]. If the stretched length is less than 2.0 cm, microphallus diagno sis is made [55]. The width of the erectile tissue and its consistency should also be recorded. Hypospadias is defined by the location of a single urethral opening and classified as first degree (asymmetric on “glans”), second degree (mid-phallic shaft), third degree (junction of the phallus with scrotum), or fourth degree (perineal, closer to anus). Rarely, 46, XX infants with salt-losing CAH (21-hydroxylase deficiency) have extreme virilization classified as Prader 5 because those individuals have a penile urethra, totally fused empty scrotum, and normal-looking genitalia. They resemble male infants with cryptorchidism.

The presence of a vaginal dimple or introi tus should be recorded. Babies with complete androgen insensitivity syndrome (CAIS) have typical female-appearing external genitalia with a normally positioned urethra and a blind vaginal pouch.

In most infants with DSD, the labioscrotal folds look like two separate sacs separated by an indentation, giving a “bifid scrotum,” either flat or rounded. The labioscrotal folds may be smooth or rugated with many linear creases on the surface. The severity of poste

### 26.6.4 Challenges in the Diagnosis

### and Treatment of DSD



26.3 The most common cause of DSD in a 46, XX genetic female with the diagnosis of CAH is a 21-hydroxylase deficiency; 75% are salt losers who develop low sodium, high potas sium, and significant weight loss during the second week of life. High renin levels can identify the asymptomatic salt loser before developing clinical dehydration. 46, XY males with CAH are at increased risk for shock because their genitalia is entirely normal. In the United States, screening newborns for CAH by measuring 17-hydroxyprogesterone has protected infants from high morbidity and death.

Female gender assignment is relatively straightforward in virilized CAH genetic females because the infants have a uterus and ovaries [45]. Many 46, XX CAH individuals with a penile urethra are being raised as girls following clitoral reduction and genital recon structive surgery. In contrast, others have been raised as males and have undergone gonadec tomy and hysterectomy. There is an ongoing debate about the best choice for these females who have unambiguously male external geni talia with female internal sex structures. The International Consensus Conference on Intersex 2006 on CAH recommends that all 46, XX CAH patients be raised, female. The CAH consensus statement indicated that “there is insufficient evidence to support rear ing a 46, XX infant at Prader 5 as a male” [56]. However, in one report, the majority (10 of the 12) 46, XX individuals diagnosed with CAH, born with Prader 4 or 5 genitalia, and assigned the male gender at birth, always lived as a male [57]. Two were reassigned to the female gender in childhood but eventually self-reassigned themselves as male. At the time of the study, the age range of the men was between 35 and 69 years. Houk and Lee proposed “consideration of male assignment for 46, XX patients who have fully developed male genitalia” [57, 58]. In a third paper, Lee and Houk [44] report rare cases of individuals

Female gender assignment is also straight forward in 46, XY individuals with Complete Androgen Insensitivity (CAIS). On the other hand, gender assignment may be challenging in 46, XY infants with either isolated micro penis or associated hypospadias. The proponents of this recommenda tion appreciated the influences of intrinsic genetic forces (nature) as well as the impact of environmental factors (nurture). With this strategy, genital reconstruction was done in infancy without a prior trial of testosterone to enlarge the micropenis. The presumption was that the penile response to treatment would not accurately predict the size of the penis in adulthood. Multiple observations have challenged this approach. 46, XY infants with micropenis reared as males have reported satisfactory sexual function as adults, even though they have expressed concerns about their penile size [64–66]. Many pediatric endocrinologists believe that 46, XY infants with isolated micropenis and a fused scrotum should receive a short course of depot testosterone (25 mg IM once a month for three injections) in infancy and be reared as males. They believe that rapid increases in the length and width of the phallus are good prognosticators of future penile growth. Penile enlargement following testosterone treatment facilitates reconstruc tive surgery to correct hypospadias in 46, XY infants with micropenis and hypospadias. The outcome of these infants in adult life appears to be reasonably good, even though penis size may not be in the normal range [64–66]. In the most severely affected 46, XY males with neg ligible phallic tissue, the urologists often advise the team and parents that penile recon struction is not advisable in childhood or ado lescence due to ongoing tissue growth. However, it may be possible in adulthood if requested.

Gender assignment is an “imperfect art” [50]. In the past, it was based solely on anec dotal evidence and “the medical team’s fear of the worst outcome”. It was also based on the notion that feminizing surgery has better long-term outcomes, a belief that is being challenged. Early feminizing genitoplasty has been criticized by some women who com plained of genital discomfort and the loss of arousability in adult life [67]. We now know that female sexual arousal and functioning are more complicated than previously assumed. It requires the preservation of erec tile tissue and neurovascular anatomy. A con structed vagina lacking intravaginal sensory

responses or the ability to lubricate will likely not result in satisfactory adult female sexual function. The reconstructive vaginoplasty may be associated with scarring of the introi tus, further affecting sexual satisfaction. Additionally, it may carry the risk of neopla sia [68]. However, it must be mentioned that female sexual arousal is even more compli cated because psychosocial factors also influ ence female sexual responses [69].

Early genital surgery may be medically indicated for infants with a urogenital sinus connecting to the upper vagina. Although some centers advocate early correction for all genital differences, vaginal reconstructive or cosmetic constructive surgery is usually done in late adolescence by an experienced surgeon. In the past, early surgery (i.e., clitoral reduc tion; hypospadias) was promoted to maximize psychosexual adjustment [70, 71]. The timing of various elective surgical procedures has become a point of great debate since they are not medically essential. One view favors the postponement of genital surgery until adoles cence [50, 67] when the person’s level of matu rity and commitment to postsurgical self-care (e.g., dilation) are crucial variables to the like lihood of a fully functional vagina. In addi tion, “nerve-sparing” clitoral surgery focusing on functional rather than cosmetic outcomes is being promoted [50].

However, the issue of no surgery, delay of surgery, or early surgery is highly controver sial. There is evidence that adverse effects of such surgery have occurred [67]. Consequently, there are proponents of performing only med ically necessary surgery for an individual’s health [50], while delaying surgery that is med ically unnecessary until adolescence, when the individual can participate in the decisionmaking along with parents and medical care takers. This position has created debate and divided the medical community with no clear consensus. Two major US children’s hospitals, Lurie Children’s Hospital in Chicago and Boston Children’s Hospital, suspended unnec essary genital surgery, especially clitoral reduction [72, 73].

There is no consensus among professionals, as shown in the recently published World Professional Association for Transgender

Health (WPATH) Standard of Care (SOC) eighth edition [74]. Those responsible for Chapter ten (Intersex) could not come to a consensus on recommendation nine, suggest ing that early genital surgery be delayed if not medically indicated. Chapter ten provides rea sons for and against this recommendation as well as published research supporting both sides. Currently, there is no long-term pro spective follow-up on individuals with a DSD diagnosis reared female who have not under gone genital surgery (e.g., clitoral reduction) or delayed surgery for the atypical external genitalia. A report summarizes ten surveys of DSD individuals (CAH 46, XX, and 47, XY reared female) who had early genital surgery [75]. The salient finding of this review is that a clear majority of patients with somatic inter sexuality favor genital surgery before the age of consent, particularly in infancy or early childhood.

Measuring outcome parameters such as vaginal width, depth, and comfort during intercourse may help resolve the early vs. later vaginal surgery debate. However, without long-term pro spective follow-up and the fact that sexual dysfunction is common in the general popu lation, it is counterintuitive that the outcome would be better if not performed in early childhood [72].

Legally, the parents decide about accept ing a female gender of rearing for a 46, XY individual and the possible need for gonadec tomy and later vaginal construction. However, religious and social beliefs may influence par ents’ decisions regarding rearing 46, XY indi viduals as males regardless of penile size or future function. Therefore, giving parents all the options regarding gender assignment, including the pros and cons of each choice, is essential. Parents must be comfortable with the gender assignment decision since they are

The level of parental understanding may require prolonged discus sions and great patience by the medical team. The trained psychologist is particularly help ful in this situation.

DSD may be present in infants with nor mal or nearly normal external genitalia [53, 76] and may be a diagnostic challenge in these infants. These include 46, XY males who have complete androgen insensitivity syndrome (CAIS) or the 17α (alpha)-hydroxylase (P450C17 or CYP17) deficiency and are born with normal female genitalia. 46, XY females with CAIS present either in childhood with a hernia containing a testis or in late adoles cence or adulthood with primary amenorrhea or absence of sexual hair. Conversely, 46, XY females with the 17α-hydroxylase deficiency may also present with primary amenorrhea. However, these individuals are hypertensive due to ACTH-mediated excess of mineralo corticoids. The latter condition is autosomal recessive, and consanguinity may be present in the parents. These patients fail to make all sex steroids because this enzyme plays a piv otal steroidogenic role in the testes and adre nal glands. Therefore, they lack sexual hair and may be misdiagnosed as having CAIS. The Sertoli cells of the testes usually function appropriately. Thus, Müllerian structures are absent, similar to individuals with CAIS. A 46, XX female with the same genetic defect lacks sex hormones and is hypertensive, but will menstruate when given estrogen replace ment because the uterus is structurally nor mal. Androgen replacement in 46, XY individuals with the 17α-hydroxylase defi ciency will result in sexual hair growth, distin guishing them clinically from individuals with CAIS.

Summarizing the data on gender change in 46, XY individuals, it is clear that gender change occurs in various frequencies in various condi tions [80]. ### 26.6.6 Gender Dysphoria Diagnosis

### and DSD

A person who desires to change gender has a Gender Identity Disorder (GID) (diagnosed by mental health professionals) using the cri teria found in the fourth version of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manuel of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV-TR). The fifth version (DSM-5) replaced the term GID with Gender Dysphoria (GD) and allowed this diagnosis to include persons with a DSD diagnosis. DSD was an exclusionary criterion for GID in DSM-IV-TR. The main reason for the change in the DSM-5 was the evidence mentioned above that self-gender change occurs in many different DSD. Perhaps another finding that was also influential but not mentioned by the DSM-5 working group was surveyed data showing that 40% of professionals working with adult transgender persons provided ser vices for people with a DSD diagnosis [83]. These professionals were members of the World Professional Association for Transgendered Health (WPATH). At the time of the survey, WPATH was the Harry Benjamin International Gender Dysphoria Association (HBIGDA). Everyone does not accept this change [84, 85] primarily because there are historical, medical, and manage ment differences between those who have Gender Dysphoria and a DSD condition and those who do not [86]. In addition to the above changes, the World Health Organization (2019) 11th edition (ICD-11) replaced older terms “Transsexualism for Adults and Adolescents and Gender Identity Disorder for Children with Gender Incongruence. Also, in ICD-11, Gender Incongruence was moved out of the “Mental and behavioral disorders” chapter and into the new “Conditions related to sexual health” chapter. This reflects current knowledge that trans-related and genderdiverse identities are not conditions of mental illness and that classifying them as such can cause enormous stigma. Unfortunately, ICD- 11 has yet to be adopted for use in the US which is using ICD-10.

### 26.6.7 Genetics and DSD

Diagnosis of patients with a DSD has tradi tionally been through assessing the patient’s phenotype, including a physical examination of the external genitalia, imaging of the inter nal reproductive structures, endocrine assess ment in basal and stimulated states, and molecular analysis of a small number of genes known to cause DSD. Modern technology, increasingly wide availability, and decreasing prices of genetic testing have allowed the genetic investigation to be utilized earlier in the diagnostic process. Potential benefits of new genetic diagnostic tools include a shorter diagnostic time, improved genetic counseling, an understanding of the family’s reproductive options, and improved monitoring [87].

### Nomenclature and Who Is Considered to Have a DSD Diagnosis

The scientific and medical communities rapidly adopted the term DSD, formulated at the Consensus meeting in Chicago and published in 2006. However, some vocal elements of the patient community strongly opposed the word “disorder”. They contend that the term “disor der” leads to an unnecessary pathologizing of atypical bodies that contribute to potentially harmful surgical “normalization” procedures [4]. Some affected individuals prefer “differ ences” to “disorders”, i.e., “differences of sex development”. Others reject DSD entirely and choose the term “intersex’ as it refers to an identity rather than a diagnosable medical con dition. The Consensus meeting was based on a global initiative to address several issues, including the nomenclature [88]. Perhaps it is not surprising that there was no resolution. Therefore, DSD can mean “disorder of sexual development” or “differences of sexual devel opment.” Then there are those who choose the descriptor intersex. There also remains dis agreement on whether conditions like Turner Syndrome, Klinefelter, and hypospadias con stitute a DSD. The CARES Foundation sup

### 26.6.9 DSD National Registries

National registries provide an extensive data base for research and clinical purposes. Categorization and standardization of infor mation allow for improved clinical care, research, and general understanding. While there have been registries for several medical conditions for years (e.g., cancer), national registries for DSD were created only recently. There are currently two such registries; the International DSD (I-DSD) in Europe and the National Institutes of Health-funded Disorders of Sex Development-Translational Research Network (DSD-TRN) in the United States [89]. Additionally, in Europe, the “dsd- LIFE” consortium consists of 16 participat ing institutions based in six different EU member states: Germany, France, UK, Sweden, The Netherlands, and Poland [90]. These registries, plus “dsd-Life”, create vast databases for research purposes. The DSD- TRN delineates the clinical care process to provide a more standardized noncategorical approach to patient and family care, thus improving outcomes [91].

A new data base has been recently estab lished in the US using electronic health records (HER) from three large Kaiser Permanente health systems to identify with high confidence that the diagnosis is correct systematically [92]. Currently, this project has identified CAH and CAIS individuals. Other DSD conditions will be added. The goal is to be able to track this large number of individu als to ascertain the type of care they are receiving.

In the United States, Short-Term Outcomes of Genitoplasty is a prospective study of the short-term outcomes of feminizing or mascu linizing surgeries for children under the age of two. There are three aims of this investigation: [1] the cosmetic outcomes of current surgical techniques to feminize or masculinize external genitalia in young children with ambiguous genitalia, [2] the incidence of urinary tract

Familial and extrafamilial experiences shape children and adolescents’ social and emotional development, especially with peers. Developing friendships and involvement in group interaction requires specific skills. The concept of “similarity” and self-perception of typicality in normative development repre sents a challenge faced by individuals with a DSD. Children and adolescents with DSD may not benefit to the same degree as other children and adolescents from experiences with peers [95]. The little evidence that exists is mixed, leading to the conclusion that the effects of DSD on psychosocial well-being and adjustment in individuals vary as a func tion of a large number of person and contextrelated variables and conditions. More multisite collaborative research is needed.

The European study (Comprehensive European Clinical Outcome Study) focuses on the long-term outcomes of adults and adoles cents with various DSD diagnoses. Another developmental task associated with enveloping positive emotional self-concept and body image is experiencing age-appropriate psychosexual milestones, including intercourse [96]. Individuals with DSD were less likely to reach each of the romantic and sexual milestones compared to their peers without a DSD and were significantly older when reaching these milestones. ### 26.6.10 Global Legislation

Legislatures in several countries have passed laws accommodating those born with a DSD and identifying as other than male or female [100]. For example, in 2012, Argentina passed the Gender Identity Law, which allows for name and sex changes on official documents without proof of surgical reconstruction or other medical or psychiatric treatment. This law was the first to allow gender reassignment based solely on an individual proclamation and applies to both DSD and transsexual cases [100]. In 2013, the Australian Senate passed the Sex Discrimination Amendment Bill [101], introducing legislation prohibiting discrimination based on DSD status. The new guidelines on gender recognition state that individuals should be given the option of selecting male, female, or intersex on their documents. In the same year, Germany intro duced an “indeterminate” sex option on birth certificates. Also, in 2013, the Council of Europe [102] adopted a resolution on the pro tection of children’s rights to physical integ rity, demanding no one be “subjected to unnecessary medical or surgical treatment that is cosmetic rather than vital for health during infancy or childhood.” In 2021, The United States issued its first U.S. passport with an “X” gender marker, acknowledging the rights of people who do not identify as male or female,

Greater emphasis on “patientcenteredness” in health care delivery and increased awareness of DSD have been accompanied by controversy, especially about the utility of elective genital surgery in infancy or early childhood. In addition, legal ques tions have been raised, and there was a lawsuit regarding the legality of surgical intervention in managing a child with a DSD [103].

### 26.6.11 Lawsuits and DSD

Individuals with a DSD, or their parents, have brought lawsuits regarding unnecessary medi cal surgery that were primarily settled out of the public’s eye [104]. In the United States, the first public lawsuit challenging early sex assignment surgery on a child with a DSD

### 26.6.12 A Shift in Research Focus

### and Clinical Care

For years, the clinical research and manage ment of persons with DSD focused on the relationship between gender identity, gender role, sexual orientation, sexual functioning, and pre- and postnatal hormones because there was evidence from animal research that hormones, primarily androgens, affected sex ual behavior. The reviews above mirror that focus. They do not report the quality of life for individuals regardless of gender assign ment and rearing or even in those who changed gender. If such data are available, they are insufficient to draw meaningful con clusions. They also do not focus on the influ ence of genes on brain sexual differentiation and development, an area that is only begin ning to be explored [105].

This approach was categorical, meaning that the focus was on investigating and managing psychosexual development and hormones to exclude other variables of human development (i.e., quality-of-life variables, in addition to psy chosexual status and hormones). Later, Sandberg and Mazur outlined an approach encompassing psychosexual development in persons with a DSD in a broader, noncategori cal approach to clinical care [91]. This noncate gorical approach guides clinical care by considering a growing body of evidence. It sup ports the notion that the successful developmen tal trajectories of persons with chronic medical conditions are influenced by the psychosocial environment, support systems, and healthcare delivery organization, in addition to the specific nature of the person’s medical condition [106, 107]. Such an approach holds the potential to systematically account for variability in DSD patient quality-of-life outcomes and translate clinical interventions, proven effective in manag ing other chronic conditions, to the circum stances faced by those affected by DSD.

While the investigation of basic biological mechanisms will continue, so will the quality of life studies in individuals with various DSD syn dromes. Examples of this work include those by Wisniewski et al. [108], and Stout et al. [109] on CAH, and Schonbucker et al. [110] on sexual quality of life in 46, XY individuals with a DSD diagnosis, Mazur on a small sample of five 46, XY individuals assigned and reared female [111], and Bean’s review of MRKH [112]. Additionally, others have focused on the effects of having a child with a DSD on parenting char acteristics [113–115] and peer relations [95].

### 26.7 Summary

The current understanding of DSD and how to care for children with these conditions result from years of basic scientific investiga tion on the sexual reproductive system. This work elucidated this system’s complex sequen tial signaling networks, which involve multiple interacting molecular signals, hormone influ ences, and cross-talk between these signaling pathways.
