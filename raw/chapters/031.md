### 31.1 Introduction

Type 2 diabetes has emerged as a global epi demic and as one of the most burdensome chronic diseases of our time. It has increased at an alarming rate in young people, parallel ing the rise in obesity. Type 2 diabetes and its comorbidities are significant risk factors for the development of cardiovascular and micro vascular complications. As the risk of compli cations increases with the duration of the disease, and because youth with type 2 diabe tes have earlier complications when compared

with adults with type 2 diabetes or children with type 1 diabetes [1–5], children represent an extremely high-risk population. In addi tion, they do not sustain remission of either glucose intolerance or their type 2 diabetes using older conventional therapy like metfor min [6, 7]. The development of new preventa tive strategies and effective therapeutic modalities is most crucial. Complete charac terization of the pathogenesis of the disease remains an important step as better under standing of the disease process will lead to the emergence of better therapeutic and preventa tive strategies. This chapter describes the vari ous aspects of type 2 diabetes in youth.

### 31.2 Epidemiology of Type 2

### Diabetes in Youth

The dramatic increases continue in the inci dence of type 2 diabetes in youth, particularly in youth of color. Since 2000, the SEARCH for Diabetes in Youth study funded by the CDC and the National Institutes of Health has provided us with very important data on the epidemiologic pattern of diabetes in chil dren of the United States. Based on the most recent data from SEARCH, the prevalence of type 2 diabetes per 1000 youth aged 10–19 years increased from 0.34 (95% CI, 0.31–0.37) in 2001 to 0.46 (95% CI, 0.43–0.49) in 2009 to 0.67 (95% CI, 0.63–0.70) in 2017, an absolute increase of 0.32 per 1000 (95% CI, 0.30–0.35) and a 95.3% (95% CI, 77.0–115.4%) relative increase over a period of 16 years [8]. The greatest absolute increases were observed in non-Hispanic Black (0.85 per 1000 [95% CI, 0.74–0.97]) and Hispanic (0.57 per 1000 [95% CI, 0.51–0.64]) youth. Also, while type 1 diabetes remains the predominant form of diabetes in childhood and adolescence, type 2 diabetes is now more prevalent than type 1 diabetes in American Indian youth and ado lescent non-Hispanic Black youth. Supporting the observed increases in prevalence, over the 17 years of the SEARCH study from 2001 to 2018, the incidence of type 2 diabetes among youth aged 10–19 years increased by 5.3% per year with a peak age of diagnosis of 16 years

Similar to the United States, global pat terns indicate that the burden of type 2 dia betes is highest in youth of color and particularly in indigenous populations across the world.

### 31.3 Pathophysiology

The pathophysiology of type 2 diabetes in youth is distinct from the disease process in type 1 diabetes and is similar to that in adults with type 2 diabetes but with some distinct differences. Failure of compensatory β-cell insulin secretion in the presence of increased insulin resistance appears to be the key factor necessary for the development of type 2 dia betes. Impaired glucose tolerance is an inter mediate stage in the natural history of type 2 diabetes mellitus and is an independent pre dictor of the risk of developing type 2 diabe tes and cardiovascular disease. In adults, the rate of progression of impaired glucose toler ance to diabetes is estimated at 5–8% per year [15]; in youth, the rate of β-cell decline is accelerated with ~25–30% lost per year in one study [16].

The Restoring Insulin Secretion (RISE) Study directly compared treatment responses between youth and adults and evaluated whether early treatment of type 2 diabetes could reverse β-cell decline. In the pediatric study, 91 pubertal youth with obesity and pre diabetes or early type 2 diabetes were random ized to one of two arms—3 months of insulin glargine followed by 9 months of metformin or 12 months of metformin alone. β-cell function was measured by hyperglycemic clamp studies at baseline, 12 and 15 months, which was 3 months after cessation of treatment. Metformin alone or basal insulin followed by metformin did not halt the deterioration in β-cell function. Both the groups had signifi cantly lower clamp measured β-cell function at 12 and 15 months compared with baseline [6]. In the adult study of 132 participants, β-cell function improved during 12 months of treat ment but was not sustained at 15 months after treatment withdrawal [17]. Baseline data from RISE demonstrated that compared with adults, youth had around 50% lower insulin sensitivity using both hyperglycemic clamp and OGTT based measures. Additionally, at baseline, youth secreted more insulin compared with adults despite similar degrees of obesity and dysglyce mia and even after adjustment for the greater degree of insulin resistance observed in youth. This β-cell hyperresponse represents a funda mental difference in the pathophysiology of dysglycemia between youth and adults, and the increased demand on β-cells may lead to more rapid loss of insulin secretory function in youth with type 2 diabetes compared with adults [18].

Type 2 diabetes is a complex disease char acterized by the interaction between many genes and environmental factors. Obesity pre disposes to type 2 diabetes because of increased peripheral resistance to insulinmediated glucose uptake. While obesity is overwhelmingly the most important risk fac tor for the development of type 2 diabetes, not all children with obesity will go on to develop type 2 diabetes; furthermore, some children develop type 2 diabetes at a relatively lower body mass index percentile than others, sug gesting the involvement of other factors in disease pathogenesis.

### 31.3.1 Effect of Puberty

Many youth with type 2 diabetes present with the condition during active puberty. Puberty is associated with a transient physiological reduction in insulin sensitivity even in lean healthy children. Insulin resistance in puberty is believed to be related in part to an increase in growth hormone levels and is normally associated with a compensatory increase in insulin secretion. Insulin resistance can increase up to 50% during puberty.

### 31.3.2 Genetic Susceptibility

The heritability of type 2 diabetes is estimated to be in the range from 30% to 70% based on family and twin studies [19] and depending on the characteristics of the study populations. The high concordance rates for type 2 diabe tes in identical twins [20], the clustering of type 2 diabetes in families [21], and the increasing prevalence of type 2 diabetes among certain populations such as Native- Americans [22] also lend support to the exis tence of genetic determinants for type 2 diabetes. In the Framingham offspring study, a cohort study in which participants are pri marily Caucasian and at relatively low risk for diabetes, both parental and offspring pheno types were ascertained by direct examination. The study showed that the risk for type 2 dia betes among offspring with a single parent with diabetes was 3.5-fold higher, and if there were two parents with diabetes, the risk was sixfold higher compared with offspring with out parental diabetes [23]. In the Treatment Options for Type 2 Diabetes in Youth and Adolescents (TODAY) study, a clinical trial of therapeutic strategies for type 2 diabetes in youth, almost 60% of subjects reported at least one parent, full sibling, or half-sibling with diabetes, and almost 90% had an affected grandparent [24]. Linkage analysis studies have led to the identification of specific genes that cause monogenic forms of diabetes such as maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY). However, these rare monogenic

causes of diabetes are identified in only a small fraction of youth with diabetes.

Type 2 diabetes is polygenic and caused by the complex interaction between numerous common and rare variants and the environ ment. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) for type 2 diabetes in adults have identified over 700 genetic variants, explain ing close to 50% of type 2 diabetes heritability, but most variants have only a small to modest effect size on risk [25, 26]. The first GWAS for type 2 diabetes in youth was performed in 2015 in the Progress for Diabetes Genetics in Youth (ProDiGY) consortium, a collabora tion of TODAY, SEARCH, and T2D-GENES study groups with 3006 multiethnic youth cases and 6061 adult controls. Seven loci at genome-wide significance were identified, including a novel locus (rs10992863) in PHF2. The remaining six loci were previously reported in adults: TCF7L2, MC4R, CDC123, KCNQ1, IGFBP2, and SLC16A11 [27]. The ProDiGY consortium also performed rare variant analysis with exome sequence data, and both common and rare variants associa tions were found to contribute more to youthonset type 2 diabetes liability variance than they did to adult-onset type 2 diabetes, with a fold increase for rare variant associations and a 3·4-fold increase with common variant asso ciations [28]. These findings indicate that the genetic architecture of youth-onset type 2 dia betes overlaps with that of adults, but that there is likely a greater contribution of genet ics to disease risk in youth compared with adults.

### 31.4 Clinical Features

Given the current obesity pandemic, distin guishing between type 1 and type 2 diabetes in children can be difficult. Nevertheless, accu rate diagnosis is critical as treatment regimens, educational approaches, dietary advice, and outcomes differ markedly between the two conditions. Most children with type 2 diabetes are overweight or have obesity at the time of diagnosis and present with glucosuria, usually

without ketonuria, absent or mild polyuria and polydipsia, and little or no weight loss [29–31]. About 40% of children and adoles cents with type 2 diabetes have no symptoms on presentation but are discovered on screen ing based on risk factors such as obesity, the presence of acanthosis nigricans on physical examination, or glycosuria detected during a medical evaluation. Acanthosis nigricans is a clinical finding associated with the insulin resistance and obesity that is very often seen in children with type 2 diabetes. This cutane ous finding is characterized by velvety, hyper pigmented patches most prominent around the neck, and other intertriginous regions and is present in as many as 90% of children with type 2 diabetes [32]. Acanthosis nigricans is speculated to be a result of insulin action upon cutaneous insulin-like growth factor receptors. In adolescent girls, polycystic ovar ian syndrome (PCOS) is associated with insu lin resistance, and girls with PCOS may be at increased risk for the development of type 2 diabetes [33, 34].

### 31.4.1 Diabetic Ketoacidosis

Although most children who present with dia betic ketoacidosis (DKA) at diagnosis are subsequently found to have type 1 diabetes, children with type 2 diabetes may also present in DKA, characterized by hyperglycemia, ketosis, and acidosis. A severe clinical presen tation of polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss and fatigue, and severe dehydration similar to that seen in DKA with type 1 diabetes can confuse the diagnosis of type 2 diabetes. In a review of children followed in the SEARCH for Diabetes in Youth study, 35.3% of youth with T1D pre sented with DKA in the United States, whereas DKA was identified in 5.5% of youth with type 2 diabetes [35]. Youth with type 2 diabetes who present in DKA often come from resource poor environments and may have food insecurity [36, 37]. Additionally, lack of ready access to medical care during the COVID pandemic has been associated with an increase in DKA in these vulnerable young people at diagnosis [38]. Sometimes, the distinction of type 2 diabetes from type 1

diabetes cannot be made until months after the presentation in DKA, when insulin requirements decline and the patient can be weaned off insulin. In addition, the persis tence of C-peptide may indicate type 2 diabe tes [39]. Children in DKA typically require hospitalization with intravenous insulin replacement therapy, rehydration, and close laboratory and clinical monitoring in an intensive care setting.

### 31.4.2 Hyperosmolar

### Hyperglycemic State

Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS) is a rare and serious acute complication of type 2 diabetes. HHS is usually associated with more severe hyperglycemia than DKA but is typi cally not characterized by ketoacidosis. It is typically characterized by marked hyperglyce mia, hyperosmolarity, and severe dehydration without ketonuria or acidosis. Rapid and accurate diagnosis of HHS is important because it is associated with high morbidity and mortality if not treated appropriately [4]. One retrospective review suggested that the presence of hyperosmolality was common with type 2 diabetes in young people with DKA [40].

### 31.4.3 Screening for Type 2

### Diabetes

Type 2 diabetes is often asymptomatic at pre sentation but screening the entire adolescent population for diabetes is not cost-effective and targeted screening of high-risk groups is a more reasonable strategy. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) recommends that screening for type 2 diabetes should be considered in children who are overweight or have obesity, and have one or more additional risk factors for diabetes. These risk factors include (1) family history of type 2 diabetes in a first- or second-degree relative, (2) Native American, African American, Latino, Asian American, or Pacific Islander race/ethnicities, (3) evidence of insulin resistance or condi

tions associated with insulin resistance (acan thosis nigricans, hypertension, dyslipidemia, polycystic ovary syndrome, or small-for-gestational-age birth weight), and (4) maternal history of diabetes or gestational diabetes mellitus during the child’s gestation. The ADA recommends that screening should be initiated at the age of 10 years or at onset of puberty if puberty occurs at a younger age, and should be repeated every 3 years or more often if BMI is increasing [5].

The International Society for Pediatric and Adolescent Diabetes (ISPAD) similarly suggests risk-based screening after the onset of puberty or at 10 years of age in youth with BMI greater than the 85th percentile and one or more of the above risk factors, adding current use of weight-promoting atypical antipsychotic drugs as one of the criteria to consider screening [4]. Additionally, screening will help identify individuals with prediabetes, the term used for youth who are at high risk for future development of diabetes and includes chil dren with impaired fasting glucose and impaired glucose tolerance. Such individuals may also be at increased risk for cardiovas cular disease independent of associated risk factors. This problem has been best studied in adults [41]. Whether prevention of diabe tes will actually reduce all long-term compli cations is not fully supported by a careful literature review, because of few long-term studies, but the data and pathophysiology are reasonably convincing [42].

As there are data to suggest that the onset of type 2 diabetes can be prevented or delayed in some individuals, screening can lead to a potential therapeutic intervention in youth who are at high risk for the development of the disease [43]. With the limited evidence base that we have related to diabetes preven tion in children, lifestyle intervention with the goal to prevent weight gain should be recom mended for all children at risk for the develop ment of type 2 diabetes. There is insufficient evidence and lack of long-term safety data in children to recommend the use of medica tions to prevent or delay the onset of diabetes. However, as data accrue on the effect of newer

obesity treatments including obesity drugs and bariatric surgery, this recommendation may change [4, 44].

### 31.5 Diagnosis of Type 2 Diabetes

### in Youth

The ADA criteria for the diagnosis of both type 2 diabetes and prediabetes in children are essentially the same as in adults. The criteria for the diagnosis of type 2 diabetes in youth is the presence of one of the following condi tions: (1) fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dl (7.0 mmol/L), (2) 2 h plasma glucose ≥200 mg/ dl (11.1 mmol/L) with a 75 g oral glucose tol erance test (OGTT), (3) HbA1C ≥ 6.5%, or (4) a random plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dl in a patient with classic symptoms of hyperglyce mia or hyperglycemic crisis. Other than in the case of unequivocal hyperglycemia, the results should be confirmed by repeat testing. Similar to adults, the criteria for the diagnosis of pre diabetes in youth is the presence of one of the following reasons: (1) fasting plasma glucose 100 mg/dl (5.6 mmol/L) to 125 mg/dl (6.9 mmol/L), (2) 2 h plasma glucose 140 mg/ dl (7.8 mmol/L) to 199 mg/dl (11.0 mmol/L) during a 75 g OGTT, or (3) HbA1C 5.7–6.4%. Prediabetes may be transient in some young people with obesity, regressing when the insu lin resistance associated with puberty lessens [4, 5].

It is critical to take race/ethnicity and the presence of anemia and hemoglobinopathies into consideration when using HbA1C to diagnose type 2 diabetes as these factors may influence the value obtained. Also, it is impor tant to understand that the studies that led to the recommendation that the HbA1C be used as a diagnostic criterion were almost entirely carried out in adult populations, and there fore, it remains somewhat unclear if the use of the HbA1C or the same HbA1C cut point is appropriate in the pediatric population [45].

The ADA in its guidelines acknowledges the limited data supporting HbA1C for diag nosing type 2 diabetes in children and adoles cents, but still recommends it for diagnosis because of the ease of obtaining a HbA1C

The diagnostic HbA1C should not be performed on a pointof-care device and should be done in a labora tory that uses a method that is certified by the National Glycohemoglobin Standardization Program. Unless there are clear clinical fea tures (e.g., marked hyperglycemia at the time of diagnosis), a test on a second blood sample is generally required for the confirmation of diabetes.

It is important to distinguish between type 1 and type 2 diabetes because the overall treat ment and long-term management strategies differ between the two diseases. Unfortunately, no single laboratory result can unequivocally distinguish between type 1 and type 2 diabetes and the distinction has to be made using a combination of clinical findings, laboratory studies, and medical judgment over time. Also, overweight or obesity can no longer be used to distinguish type 1 and type 2 diabetes because of the increasing prevalence of obe sity in all youth including those with type 1 diabetes [46]. Youth with type 2 diabetes gen erally have obesity or are overweight, typically present during puberty and show clinical evi dence of insulin resistance in the form of acanthosis nigricans. These children typically also have an immediate family history of type 2 diabetes and many identify as youth of color. Hyperketonemia or ketonuria are infre quently associated with type 2 diabetes and are easy and rapid laboratory markers, although not very specific. The presence of serum pancreatic autoantibodies is confirma tory of type 1 diabetes, and these are probably the most useful laboratory measures to differ entiate between the two conditions. ### 31.6 Treatment of Type 2 Diabetes

### in Youth

As in adults, treatment goals for youth with type 2 diabetes include achieving and main taining near-normal glycemia and preventing and reducing the risk of long-term vascular complications, as well the appropriate man agement of comorbidities including hyperten sion, dyslipidemia, and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Because of the aggres sive nature of the disease and early onset of long-term complications, a comprehensive approach to treatment of youth-onset type 2 diabetes is recommended. The management team for type 2 diabetes should ideally include a pediatric endocrinologist, certified diabetes educator, a nutritionist, a psychologist and/or social worker, and an exercise physiologist all of whom should provide consistent education to families [4, 51]. Diabetes education should be provided in a culturally sensitive and age appropriate manner with the use of inter preter or translator services if needed and should be specific to type 2 diabetes.

Overall, the guidelines recommend met formin in addition to lifestyle intervention if HbA1C at diagnosis is <8.5%. If HbA1C ≥ 8.5%, insulin is required in addition to metformin and basal insulin is typically ini tiated at doses up to 0.5 units/kg/day. Typically, insulin can be weaned over 1–2 months as the metformin dose is titrated with a goal to elim inate insulin therapy, which is weight gain promoting, while maintaining HbA1C in the

target range. Subsequent treatment requires targeting a HbA1C of <7% or ideally <6.5% with the addition of second- or third-line agents as necessary. In the presence of meta bolic decompensation, inpatient admission and initiation of intravenous insulin similar to type 1 diabetes protocols are required.

The TODAY study is a landmark multi center clinical trial that evaluated therapeutic options for type 2 diabetes in youth between the ages of 10 and 17 years. All the partici pants were initially treated with metformin alone and received standard diabetes educa tion focused on healthy eating and exercise to attain a glycated hemoglobin level of <8%. Subsequently, 699 children were randomized to continue treatment with 1000 mg twice a day of metformin alone, metformin combined with 4 mg twice a day of rosiglitazone, or an intensive lifestyle intervention program that focused on weight loss. The mean follow-up period for the study was 3.8 years, and the pri mary outcome was loss of glycemic control (defined as a HbA1C of greater than or equal to 8%) for at least 6 months or sustained met abolic decompensation requiring insulin for 3 months or more. The study showed that metformin plus rosiglitazone was superior to metformin alone (P = 0.006) and that treat ment with metformin plus lifestyle interven tion was not significantly different from treatment with metformin alone, with failure rates of 51.7%, 38.6%, and 46.6% in the met formin, metformin plus rosiglitazone, and metformin plus lifestyle intervention arms, respectively. Overall, 45.6% of study partici pants experienced treatment failure irrespec tive of treatment regimen, and the median time to treatment failure was 11.5 months. Treatment failure rates in youth were higher than in comparable adult studies, and the fail ure of the lifestyle arm to make major improvement in metabolic control was differ ent from previous studies in adults with pre diabetes or early diabetes [52, 53]. The TODAY study laid the initial framework for how we think about managing type 2 diabetes in youth and its outcomes confirmed the need for new pharmacologic and surgical approaches to this disorder.

### 31.6.1 Lifestyle Modification

Nonpharmacological measures of diet and physical activity aimed at weight reduction are cornerstones for type 2 management in youth in conjunction with pharmacotherapy. Although difficult to achieve over the long term, weight loss improves glycemic control and the underlying insulin resistance and should be an essential part of the successful management of type 2 diabetes in children. The current literature is inconclusive about a single best meal strategy for patients with dia betes, and there are very few studies address ing this issue in children. Youth should be referred to a registered dietitian who has expe rience in the nutritional needs of youth with type 2 diabetes.

Regardless of the type of meal plan pre scribed, nutritional education must be pro vided to the patient and the family to maximize adherence and to obtain positive results. This education should encourage patients to follow some basic healthy eating patterns, such as eating regular meals and snacks, and not eat ing while watching television or while using the computer or other electronic gadgets [54]. Common dietary recommendations should also stress eliminating sugar sweetened soft drinks and juices, limiting portion sizes, reducing the consumption of foods made from refined, simple sugars and high fructose corn syrup, limiting intake of high-fat and/or calorie dense foods, and reducing fast food and prepackaged food consumption. A family-centered approach to nutrition and lifestyle modification is crucial in children with type 2 diabetes, and nutrition recommen dations should be culturally appropriate and sensitive to the financial structure and needs of the family [4] .

Physical activity is an essential component of lifestyle education for the management of type 2 diabetes in youth. Providers should encourage youth to engage in at least 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity daily [55] and to limit screen time, which con tributes to a sedentary lifestyle, to <2 h per day [56]. Physical activity should be promoted as a family event, and common day-to-day

activities such as using stairs instead of eleva tors, walking to school, and doing housework or yard work should be discussed.

### 31.6.2 Pharmacological Agents

Metformin and insulin were for many years the only two agents approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (US FDA) for the treatment of type 2 diabetes in children. A number of new agents are now approved for the treatment of type 2 diabetes in children and in adults, and they seem to be more efficacious than metformin in achieving glycemic goals. Whether this efficacy will per sist over the lifetime of this condition is not yet clear.

5 Metformin

Metformin is the first-line line pharma cologic therapy for treatment of type 2 dia betes in children. The guidelines recommend that metformin can be initi ated along with lifestyle intervention in all children with type 2 diabetes (unless insu lin is required) because of the low success rate of lifestyle intervention alone in pedi atric patients. Metformin is a biguanide that largely acts by decreasing hepatic glu cose output by inhibiting gluconeogenesis. In addition, metformin increases insulinmediated glucose utilization in the periph eral tissues. This is done by altering the energy metabolism in the cell, primarily by mitochondrial inhibition. The molecular mechanism of action of metformin is not completely understood but it is thought to be mediated through activation of the enzyme 5′ adenosine monophosphate kinase, which in addition to modulating insulin signaling is also thought to have positive downstream effects on lipid metabolism [57].

5 Insulin

Metformin can cause some weight loss on initiation of treatment but is overall considered as a weight neutral drug. The most bothersome side effects seen with metformin therapy are gastrointestinal and include abdominal pain, bloating, change in appetite, and diarrhea. These adverse effects are often transient and dis

appear with continuation of the medica tion. Metformin is generally better tolerated when taken with meals. To mini mize gastrointestinal side effects seen with metformin therapy, the dose should be started at 500 mg once a day and increased by 500 mg every week to reach a maximum daily dose of 2000 mg a day in divided doses. Overall, metformin is considered to be a very safe drug but is contraindicated in children with liver disease, impaired renal function or in cases of cardiopulmonary dysfunction because of the small risk of lactic acidosis [58]. It should be discontinued during acute illness or before imaging studies requiring a contrast agent [59].

Metformin generally is effective in the initial phases of diabetes treatment but unfortunately in many cases, monotherapy is not effective, and so, the addition of insulin may be required. In the TODAY study, metformin alone provided durable glycemic control in approximately only half of the subjects [60], again suggesting that many youth with type 2 diabetes are likely to require combination treatment within a few years of diagnosis.

Insulin therapy is recommended during the acute presentation of type 2 diabetes or in cases of severe hyperglycemia as described earlier. In addition, a high pro portion of children with type 2 diabetes will ultimately go on to require insulin therapy. Similar to the management of type 1 diabetes, both long-acting agents, such as insulin glargine, insulin detemir, and insulin degludec as well as rapid act ing agents, such as insulin lispro and insu lin aspart, should be considered based on the individual patient, severity of disease, the ability to perform self-blood glucose monitoring, and the risk of hypoglycemia. Newer long-acting insulin preparations that can be administered once weekly may soon enhance the insulin armamentarium [61].

### 31.6.3 Bariatric Surgery

Bariatric surgical procedures are effective interventions for treating type 2 diabetes in adults who have extreme obesity and are refractory to medical therapy. Improvement in metabolic control is often evident within days to weeks following Roux-en-Y gastric bypass surgery, most likely reflecting an alter ation in metabolism that is independent of weight loss [79]. At present, there is a limited but increasing body of literature to support the use of bariatric surgery for carefully selected adolescents with extreme obesity. Diabetic adolescents with extreme obesity experience significant weight loss and similar rapid remission of type 2 diabetes mellitus after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass with improve ments in insulin resistance, β-cell function, and cardiovascular risk factors [80]. Bariatric surgery in adolescents and youth with type 2 diabetes was more effective than medical ther apy provided to participants in the TODAY study consisting of metformin, metformin plus intensive lifestyle management, or met formin with rosiglitazone [81].

Although bariatric surgery still seems superior to pharmacotherapy for weight loss and diabetes remission in youth with obesity and type 2 diabetes, newer drug regimens may be effective, and combined regimens of sur gery and pharmacotherapy may prove to be even more effective in the future.

### 31.6.5 Treatment Monitoring

Guidelines from the ADA recommend that the target HbA1C should be <7%. Patients should have individualized short-term goals with the ultimate goal of reaching the target HbA1C. Diabetes therapy should be intensi fied if possible, when the HbA1C is above tar get. In patients who are on insulin therapy, HbA1C measurement should be done every 3 months, and fingerstick blood glucose mon itoring should be performed at least three times daily. For other patients using noninsu lin therapy and in those with stable HbA1C values that are within the target range, HbA1C measurement may only be required every 6 months. In such patients, fasting and 2-h postprandial fingerstick blood glucose levels after the largest meal of the day, a few times a week, can provide valuable information on the range of glycemic excursion. Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) can be considered for some young people with type 2 diabetes who are on complicated insulin regimens, but utility of CGM for this population is still unclear [4, 5].

### 31.7 Comorbidities of Type 2

### Diabetes in Youth

In many cases, comorbidities are already pres ent at the time of diagnosis in youth with type 2 diabetes. Youth with type 2 diabetes are at increased risk for other obesity associated dis orders including hypertension, dyslipidemia, and NAFLD. These increase the already high risk for long-term vascular and other compli cations. Therefore, it is crucial to appropri ately manage them in conjunction with the diabetes.

### 31.7.1 Hypertension

Hypertension increases the risk of future car diovascular disease risk and renal disease in youth with type 2 diabetes. The TODAY study found that at enrollment, 11.6% of partici pants had hypertension with a mean age of 14 ± 2 years and mean duration of diabetes of 7.8 ± 5.8 months. The prevalence of hyperten sion increased to 33.8% by the end of the study, with an average follow-up of 3.9 years [82]. The SEARCH study found that hyper tension was present in 65% of 95 youth with type 2 diabetes of mean disease duration of 2.1 (SD 1.6) years enrolled in the study between 2001 and 2003 [83]. Blood pressure should be measured accurately and reviewed at each visit. Both systolic and diastolic blood pressures should be below the 90th percentile for age, gender, and height (or below 120/80, whichever is lower) according to the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute standards. The initial treatment of elevated blood pres sure should consist of efforts at lifestyle changes aimed at weight loss and dietary changes consistent with the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet which includes increased servings of fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, limited added sugars, and a dietary sodium intake of <2.3 g a day [84]. If there is persistent eleva tion of blood pressure after 6 months of life style modifications, pharmacological management should be initiated. Angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors or angio tensin receptor blockers (ARBs) are generally recommended for the initial treatment of hypertension because they reduce the risk of progressive renal disease. Both ACEs and ARBs are potentially teratogenic and so the risks must be discussed, and contraceptive guidance provided for adolescent females. In cases when adequate control of hypertension is not achieved, referral to a specialist trained in the treatment of hypertension in youth is recommended, which should include evalua tion for secondary causes of hypertension [85].

### 31.7.2 Dyslipidemia

The typical pattern of dyslipidemia in youth with type 2 diabetes is characterized by the presence of high plasma triglyceride levels, low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels, and an increased concentra tion of small, dense low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) particles which are ath erogenic [86]. In the TODAY study, at base line, 4.5% of participants had LDL-C levels that were >130 mg/dl or were already receiv ing lipid-lowering therapy. After 36 months of follow-up, the prevalence of youth with ele vated LDL-C levels or on lipid-lowering agents increased to 10.7%. At baseline, 21% of youth had elevated triglyceride levels and this number rose to 23.3% after 3 years [87]. The observational SEARCH study found that 65% of youth with type 2 diabetes had hyper triglyceridemia and 60% of youth had a low HDL-C level [88]. Youth with type 2 diabetes have high rates of dyslipidemia and diabetesrelated complications [3], and so, LDL-C treatment thresholds are more aggressive than for youth with type 1 diabetes.

### 31

Current recommendations by the ADA are that, at the initial evaluation, all the patients with type 2 diabetes should have a baseline complete fasting lipid profile after initial glycemic control has been established or 3 months after initiation of treatment regardless of glycemic control achieved, with follow-up testing based on these findings, or annually thereafter if initial results are nor mal [51]. The guidelines suggest that if the ini tial LDL-C concentration is greater than or equal to 130 mg/dl, attempts should be made to optimize HbA1C, and dietary counselling by a nutritionist should be provided. LDL-C measurements should be repeated after 6 months, and if levels are still between 130 and 160 mg/dl, statin therapy should be initi ated, with a goal LDL-C of <130 mg/dl and an ideal target of <100 mg/dl. Lovastatin, simvastatin, pravastatin, rosuvastatin, and atorvastatin are the statins approved by the US FDA for use in children. Treatment should be initiated with the lowest recommended dose for each statin and titrated based on

LDL-C measurements and side effects. As an example, atorvastatin can be initiated at 5 mg and the dose can be titrated upwards as needed to the maximum daily dose. The maxi mum daily dose for atorvastatin in adults is 80 mg per day but a dose of only up to 20 mg per day has been examined in pediatric clinic trials. Similarly, fluvastatin can be initiated at 20 mg and titrated upwards to a maximum dose of 80 mg per day [89]. Some side effects that are common to many of the statins include muscle injury, hepatic, and renal dys function. It is also very important to note that statins are teratogenic and therefore should not be used in the absence of effective contra ception in adolescent girls who are sexually active. For triglycerides, if the initial concen trations are between 150 and 600 mg/dl, patients should be given appropriate dietary guidance. If levels are between 700 and 1000 mg/dl at the initial or follow-up visit, fibrate or niacin therapy should be considered if the patient is older than 10 years because of the increased risk of pancreatitis at these con centrations. Although there are limited data on the use of fibric acid derivatives in chil dren, agents such as gemfibrozil and fenofi brate are used in cases of severe hypertriglyceridemia. Gemfibrozil is typically administered at a dose of 1200 mg a day and fenofibrate can be initiated at 40 mg and titrated upwards to close to 200 mg based on the preparation used. Niacin is rarely used because of multiple intolerable side effects that include flushing, abdominal pain, vomit ing, headache, and elevated serum amino transferase levels [90].

NAFLD may progress to cirrhosis and liver failure and is the most com mon cause of chronic liver disease in youth. ### 31.7.4 Depression

Depression is a significant comorbidity of type 2 diabetes in children and can complicate the medical management of diabetes and adherence to treatment. The identification of depression with the use of appropriate screen ing tools evaluating for depression, diabetes distress, and disordered eating and referral to a specialist when necessary, should be essen tial components of type 2 diabetes manage ment in youth.

### 31.8 Complications of Type 2

### Diabetes in Youth

Type 2 diabetes is a major risk factor for the future development of cardiovascular disease. The microvascular complications of type 2 diabetes include retinopathy, nephropathy, and both peripheral and autonomic neuropa thy. The follow-up of the TODAY study dem onstrated a complication rate much higher than expected in the adult population, stress

ing that the youth population with type 2 dia betes is epidemiologically and biologically different and at greater risk than adults with the disorder at an older age. There was a con tinuing increase in microvascular complica tions over approximately 13 years of study and follow-up, with at least 60% of partici pants in this study manifesting at least one complication by the time of last follow-up. Complications included retinopathy, nephrop athy, neuropathy, and macrovascular disease [94].

### 31.8.1 Retinopathy

Diabetic retinopathy can lead to impaired vision and visual loss. Results from both the TODAY and SEARCH studies indicate that there is a substantial prevalence of diabetic retinopathy in these young people. In the TODAY study, eye examinations with retinal photographs were taken in 517 subjects, after they had had diabetes for 2 and 8 years. Nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) was present in 13.9% of participants. The prevalence of retinopathy increased with age, duration of diabetes, and mean HbA1C [95]. After a mean diabetes duration of 12 years, 49% of the participants in this study (mean age 25.4 years) had retinopathy. At this point, 6% had moderate to severe NPDR and 3.8% had proliferative diabetic retinopathy. The major correlate with severity of retinopa thy remained in the HbA1C level [96]. Similarly, SEARCH evaluated the prevalence of retinopathy in 447 young people with type 2 diabetes with mean disease duration of 12 years using nonmydriatic retinal photogra phy of both the eyes. The prevalence of dia betic retinopathy was 52% for 2159 subjects with type 1 diabetes and 56% for subjects with type 2 diabetes. Average HbA1C was signifi cantly higher in subjects with diabetic retinop athy compared with subjects without diabetic retinopathy and an increase in HbA1C during follow-up was also associated with progres sion of retinopathy [97].

The ADA provides recommendations for the identification and management of diabetic

retinopathy in adolescents with type 2 diabe tes. Patients should have an initial dilated and comprehensive eye examination performed by an ophthalmologist or optometrist shortly after diabetes diagnosis. Subsequent examina tions by an ophthalmologist should be repeated annually and less frequent examina tions may be considered after 1 or more nor mal eye examinations. Providers should promptly refer patients with any retinal changes to an ophthalmologist who is knowl edgeable and experienced in the management and treatment of diabetic retinopathy.

### 31.8.2 Nephropathy

Diabetic nephropathy is a complication of type 2 diabetes and a major cause of chronic renal disease in adults. Moderately increased albuminuria, formerly called “microalbumin uria” is defined as urinary albumin excretion between 30 and 300 mg/day or between 30 and 300 mg/g creatinine on a random urine sam ple, and severely increased albuminuria, for merly called “macroalbuminuria” is defined as urinary albumin excretion above 300 mg/day or above 300 mg/g creatinine on a random urine sample. Moderately increased albumin uria precedes the development of severely increased albuminuria and is considered to predict high risk for future nephropathy. In the TODAY cohort, moderately increased albuminuria was found in 6.3% of partici pants at baseline and rose to 16.6% by the end of study, regardless of treatment arm. Glycemic control was the only significant pre dictor of risk of microalbuminuria [82, 98]. The SEARCH study also evaluated the pres ence of increased albumin to creatinine ratio in 2885 children with type 1 diabetes with a mean duration of diabetes of 3.7 years and 375 youth with type 2 diabetes with a mean duration of diabetes of 1.9 years. The preva lence of elevated albumin to creatinine ratio was 2.3 times higher in youth with type 2 dia betes (22.2%, 95% CI 18.3–26.7) when com pared with youth with type 1 diabetes (9.2%, 95% CI, 8.2–10.3) [99].

According to the ADA, screening for albu minuria with a random spot urine sample for

albumin to creatinine ratio should begin at the time of type 2 diabetes diagnosis and be repeated annually. Albumin excretion is influ enced by various factors including exercise and can vary from day to day. Therefore, an abnormal value should be repeated. Orthostatic proteinuria is not uncommon in adolescents and is usually considered benign. Therefore, all patients with documented albu minuria should have a first morning urine void immediately on waking to determine whether albuminuria is benign. Persistent albuminuria requires referral to a pediatric nephrologist for further management. An ini tial treatment of persistent albuminuria is with an ACE inhibitor and should be initi ated, even if the blood pressure is not elevated. Enalapril, fosinopril, and lisinopril are among the ACE inhibitors FDA approved for use in children. Typically, treatment is initiated with 5 mg of enalapril or 2.5 mg of lisinopril a day and can be titrated upwards as needed. In the case of side effects such as persistent cough that are associated with ACE inhibitors, an ARB such as Losartan can be used at an ini tial dose of 25 mg a day. Both ACE inhibitors and ARBs are teratogenic, and adolescent girls should be counseled on effective contra ception before use. Further monitoring of albumin levels should be at 3–6-month inter vals to assess both the patient’s response to therapy and disease progression with the goal to achieve as normal an albumin-to-creatinine ratio as possible.

### 31.8.3 Neuropathy

Diabetic neuropathy includes both peripheral and autonomic neuropathy. The prevalence of neuropathy has not been systematically stud ied in youth with type 2 diabetes. However, similar to other complications, the risk of dia betic neuropathy in adults has been shown to increase with increasing duration of disease and is related to glycemic control. In a longterm follow-up of the TODAY study partici pants, the cumulative incidence of clinically measurable neuropathy was 32.4% after 15 years of follow-up [94]. ### 31.8.4 Macrovascular

### Complications

Macrovascular complications include coro nary artery, peripheral, and cerebral vascular disease and are the major sources of morbid ity and mortality in adults with type 2 diabe tes [100]. Currently, we have little formal evidence on the incidence of these complica tions in children, but they are thought to be exceedingly rare. However, results from adult studies indicate that the combination of poor glycemic control and additional risk factors such as hyperlipidemia and hypertension increase the risk of these complications. The TODAY study performed echocardiograms in 455 youth with a mean age of 18 years and a mean duration of diabetes of 4.5 years. The results showed that 16.2% of participants had adverse measures of cardiac structure and function that were positively related to BMI and blood pressure but were not related to treatment of their diabetes [101]. Longer-term follow-up 5 years after the end of the study revealed continuing evolution of subclinical cardiac dysfunction with 15.8% demonstrat ing actual cardiac structural abnormalities, as well as worsening left ventricular diastolic function. These findings continued to corre late with hypertension, obesity, worse glyce mic control, and elevated heart rate. Female sex as well as Hispanic and non-Hispanic Black ethnicity also increased risk. These findings offer preliminary evidence of the risk of cardiac complications in youth with type 2 diabetes [102]. Arterial stiffness and abnormal heart rate variability also increased with time in this population. This correlated with hyper tension and poor glycemic control. These

findings are considered preclinical markers of macrovascular disease [103]. In addition, in this young adult population at last follow-up, there were 17 reported cardiovascular events that were considered serious, including four myocardial infarctions, six cases of congestive heart failure, six individuals with coronary artery disease, and four strokes [94].

### 31.9 Conclusions

Type 2 diabetes, although still relatively uncommon in childhood, is increasing at an alarming rate in youth and is associated with significant comorbidities and diabetes compli cations. These youth are at future risk of development of long-term macrovascular and microvascular complications, which poses an enormous public health challenge. There is limited literature on disease pathogenesis in children, and although at present, therapeutic options are more effective than those in the past, duration of efficacy has still not been carefully assessed. Further studies are crucial to better understand the underlying basis of the disease in youth and for the development of effective preventative and therapeutic strat egies.
